Start here

The churn book is not a story about losing loyal members. It is a story about memberships that never really began.

Every membership ever sold at Bandra sits in this ledger: 8,707 memberships taken out by 3,768 people, of which 3,974 were paid memberships from 1,699 members. Read forward from purchase, the pattern is stark and consistent across two years of trading: one in five paid memberships ends within a week. More than half end within a month. Only 3.2% are still in force a year later.

The median paid Bandra membership lasts 19 days. Not months — days. And it has shortened: the median membership bought in 2024 ran 29 days, one bought in 2025 ran 17, and one bought so far in 2026 is running 19. Every retention, referral and frequency conversation the studio has depends on that number, and it is getting worse.

The reassuring reading is in how people are lost rather than how many. Among memberships with enough visits to measure a trend, 37.2% ran a second half that was half the first or worse — and 10.5% ended with a second half in which the member did not attend at all. They did not cancel, complain or ask for a refund. They drifted, while the membership was still live and still contactable. That is a recoverable failure, and it is what this report is built around.

The shape of the churn book

93.5% of every membership Bandra has ever sold is now lapsed. The live book is a thin sliver of the lifetime book.

At Bandra the derived statuses read: 7,745 lapsed (89.0%), 371 in a renewal grace period, 352 never activated, 111 active and 77 renewed.

That ratio is less alarming than it looks, because it counts every instance since 2022 including free and promotional entries — 4,519 of the 8,707 rows are complimentary or staff entries rather than purchases. Restricted to paid memberships the picture softens but does not change shape: of 3,974 paid memberships, 89.7% are lapsed and only 8.2% are live or renewing.

What a 43.0% thirty-day survival rate means commercially

A studio that loses 57.0% of its memberships inside a month is not running a retention business. It is running an acquisition business with a very short billing cycle — and every month it must refill 57.0% of the book from scratch simply to stand still. That is the reason the studio’s lead engine has to run as hard as it does, and it reframes acquisition from growth activity into maintenance cost.

The two ways to lose a member

Half the churn is people who never engaged. Half is people who engaged and then withdrew. They need opposite responses.

Sort the paid memberships by how often they cancelled and a useful split appears. 2,084 memberships never cancelled a single class — and they completed an average of just 2.3 sessions. They did not leave because something went wrong; they never arrived. At the other end, 467 memberships cancelled more than half of everything they booked, averaging 10.8 late cancellations each. Those members were present, engaged, and visibly losing the habit before they stopped.

Two failure modes, two fixes. The first is a first-fortnight activation problem — the product was bought and not used. The second is a habit-decay problem — the rhythm broke. A single win-back campaign aimed at both will be efficient at neither, which is why the recommendations in this report keep them apart.

Did they use what they paid for?

Most did. The ones who did not are identifiable at the point of sale, and they are carrying a lot of unspent money.

Session allowance is recorded for 802 package and credit memberships. Of those, 332 (41.4%) used every class they bought, and a further 103 used more than three-quarters. At the other end, 145 memberships (18.1%) used a quarter or less, leaving 1,953 unused classes in total.

The unused-credit group is the one to act on, commercially as much as humanly. A member who has paid and not attended has received nothing for their money; the studio has banked revenue it will eventually have to earn or give back in goodwill. It is also a countable, visible, unfinished transaction sitting in the CRM: memberships in the lowest usage band are carrying 8 and 7 unspent classes.

The decline that comes first

Almost every lost member slowed down before they stopped. The slowdown is measurable, and it happens while the membership is still live.

Splitting each membership period in half and comparing the two shows the shape of a departure. 10.5% of measurable memberships ended with a second half in which the member did not attend at all, and a further 26.7% more than halved their frequency. Together, 37.2% of memberships ran a second half that was half the first or worse, against just 36.4% that held or grew.

This is a warning the studio already owns and does not use. The decline is visible from the mid-point of any monthly or quarterly cycle. A member whose visits halve in the second fortnight of a one-month membership is stating exactly what is about to happen, and nothing in the current process reads that signal.

Product by product

Commitment length is the only product feature that reliably predicts survival. Price is not.

Sorted by renewal rate the products separate cleanly. The 6-month unlimited renews at 40%, the extended 10-class pack at 22% and the 12-class package at 13%. At the bottom, the 2-for-1 introductory pack renews at 5%, the four-class package at 4% and the ten-class single pack at 1%.

Two things stand out. The 10-class single pack is the worst performer in the range — it sells at almost the same ticket as the 12-class package and converts to a renewal 12 points less often. And the gap between the two is not price; it is whether the member was sold a programme or a quantity.

Who is winnable

The win-back list writes itself, and it is the opposite of the list most studios use.

Sorted by how long it has been since they last came, the paid book splits into three groups with very different economics. 316 members are still warm — in the room within the last sixty days — and they paid an average of ₹23,011 across 14.4 completed classes. A further 378 are cooling, having paid ₹11,862. The remaining 1301 are gone, absent more than six months, and paid only ₹9,525 on average.

The instinct is to chase whoever spent the most over their lifetime. The data says the opposite: the members still worth winning are the ones who have been gone the least time, and they are also the ones who were worth the most. Someone last seen three weeks ago who has paid ₹23,011 is a lapse. Someone last seen two years ago who paid ₹9,525 is a completed relationship.

What the lost book cost

The lapsed memberships represent almost all of the money the studio has taken at Bandra, and most of it was spent fast.

The 3,974 paid Bandra memberships in this ledger represent ₹4,40,74,830 of recorded payments, at an average ticket of ₹11,091. Of that, ₹3,44,72,653 — 78.2% — sits against memberships now recorded as lapsed. That is not money the studio has lost; it is money the studio has already banked against relationships that have ended.

The sharper question is how quickly it was earned. With a median membership lasting 19 days and 57.0% finished inside a month, an average ticket of ₹11,091 is being collected roughly every 19 days and then having to be replaced. The studio is not short of customers. It is short of duration — and duration is the one input that makes every other number in these reports easier.

Recommendations

Nine actions, ordered by the size of the leak they address
01

Run a fourteen-day activation sequence on every membership sold

Studio Manager + CRM

Reasoning42.7% of paid memberships end within fourteen days and 57.0% within thirty, and 2,084 memberships never cancelled a single class because they never booked one. The median paid membership at Bandra lasts 19 days.

Expected impactGetting one in ten of the memberships that currently die inside a fortnight to reach four visits would add roughly 307 members into the active habit band each year. On the studio’s own attendance economics that is the largest single recoverable block in this report.

02

Treat "bought and never attended" as a same-day alert, not a report line

Front Desk + CRM

ReasoningThere are 352 Bandra memberships across 241 people that were paid for and never activated, and the trend is rising: 36 in 2024, 81 in 2025, 235 so far in 2026.

Expected impactA member who has bought and not booked is the highest-intent, lowest-cost call the studio can make. Ringing them within 48 hours of purchase converts a dead transaction into a first visit, and a first visit is the only thing that has ever predicted a second.

03

Put a member on a plan at their first renewal, not a quantity

Studio Manager

ReasoningThe 12-class package renews at 13% while the 10-class single pack renews at 1%, at virtually the same ticket. The 4-class package renews at 4% and the 2-for-1 pack at 5%.

Expected impactThe difference is not price, it is framing. Selling the same money as a scheduled programme rather than a bundle of classes is a script change, not a product change, and it moves the single largest cohort in the book.

04

Write down the unused classes and go and collect them

Studio Manager + CRM

Reasoning1,953 paid classes sit unused across 802 memberships, and 145 memberships used a quarter or less of what they bought. Memberships that used 10% or less of their allowance are carrying 8 unspent classes each.

Expected impactEvery one of those classes has already been paid for. Asking a member to come and use what they own is the least expensive contact the studio can make, it requires no discount and no offer, and it puts a body back in the room.

05

Watch the second half of every membership for the halving signal

CRM

Reasoning37% of memberships show a second half that was half the first or worse, and 10% end with a second half in which the member did not attend at all. Only 36% held or improved.

Expected impactThis is a leading indicator that already exists in the data and is not being read. A member whose visit rate halves in the middle of a membership should trigger a human contact while the membership is still live, not a win-back campaign after it ends.

06

Segment win-back by recency, not by lifetime value

CRM + Marketing

Reasoning316 members were last in the room within 60 days and paid an average of ₹23,011; 1301 have been gone over six months and paid ₹9,525.

Expected impactThe instinct is to chase the biggest historical spenders. The evidence is that recency and value travel together: the recently lapsed are both the most winnable and the most valuable. Re-ranking the win-back list by recency would re-point the same effort at roughly double the average ticket.

07

Separate the two churn mechanisms in every dashboard

CRM + Studio Manager

Reasoning2,084 memberships never booked a class, while 467 cancelled more than half of everything they booked. Both are currently reported as one lapsed number.

Expected impactAn onboarding problem and a habit-decay problem need different owners, different scripts and different timelines. Reporting them as a single churn figure guarantees that the studio treats both with the remedy for whichever is more visible.

08

Freeze, do not lose, the members who are slipping

Front Desk

ReasoningOnly 809 of 3,974 paid memberships ever used a freeze, while 241 members never activated at all and 2,084 never booked.

Expected impactA two-week freeze costs the studio almost nothing, keeps the contract alive, and converts a lapse into a pause. Offering it proactively at the moment attendance drops is cheaper than any win-back campaign and protects the relationship while the member sorts out whatever interrupted it.

09

Price the churn into the acquisition plan

Management

ReasoningAt a 57.0% thirty-day and 76.1% sixty-day end rate, the studio must replace most of its book every two months. That is the real reason the lead engine has to run at 7,822 leads.

Expected impactFraming acquisition as the cost of churn rather than as growth changes what the studio is willing to spend on retention. On this data, a one-point improvement in thirty-day survival removes the need for more acquisition than any campaign the studio could run.

10

Sell the first month as a programme, not a class count

Sales + Front Desk

ReasoningAcross 922 Bandra first-timers with a recorded first purchase, the product they started on predicts almost the whole of their lifetime value. A member whose first purchase was a 1 Month Unlimited is worth ₹75,012 on average; one whose first purchase was a single class is worth ₹13,248. Same studio, same period, same marketing — a 5.7-fold difference decided at the point of sale.

Expected impactThe studio’s entry offer is the cheapest thing it can say yes to, and the cheapest thing is producing the least valuable members. Selling the first month as a timed programme with a named start date, rather than a pack of classes the member can spend at leisure, changes the product without changing the price. The trial class should be an audition for ₹75,012, not a coupon for one session.

11

Book the second visit before the member leaves the building

Front Desk + CRM

ReasoningAmong 1,445 measurable first-timers, lifetime value falls in a straight line with how long the second visit took. Members back within a day are worth ₹43,407 and are retained at 86%. Those whose second visit took a month or never happened are worth ₹13,941 and retained at 96%.

Expected impactThe second visit is worth 3.1 times the third, the tenth or anything after it, because everything after it depends on it. It is also the easiest thing in this report to influence: a member standing at the desk on their first day will say yes to a date. Nobody is currently asking.

12

Read the at-risk band fortnightly, not the lapsed band annually

Studio Manager + CRM

ReasoningOf every Bandra first-timer on file, 2,927 are recorded as churned and 622 as lapsed, against 383 active and only 165 flagged at risk. The members in the at-risk band are worth ₹19,265 each on average against ₹3,857 for the churned band.

Expected impactThe studio has one band where intervention is cheap and one where it is expensive, and the report it reads monthly is weighted towards the second. Moving the same effort one stage earlier — from the members who have already gone to the members whose visits have just slipped — multiplies the yield of every retention hour the team spends.
