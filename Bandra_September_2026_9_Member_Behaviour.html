Member Behaviour and Patterns  ·  September 2026

Supreme HQ, Bandra — how members actually behave

394 members trained at Bandra in September 2026, 1,404 times between them. This report is about the shape of that behaviour rather than its size: when members come, how long they leave between visits, how often they buy, how far they get before they stop, and which of those patterns the studio can actually change. The headline is that most of what looks like churn is a single early step that nobody owns.

Start here

Half the members came once. The most frequent tenth delivered two fifths of the attendance. And almost all of the loss happens at a single step.

The studio’s members are morning people. 249 of 394 members do most of their training before noon, against 129 who come in the evening. The busiest hours of September were 11:00 (265 check-ins) and 09:00 (252), and the busiest day was Tuesday.

They come in clusters, not on a schedule. The median gap between a member’s visits is 1 day, and 85% of every gap on record is a week or less. Bandra members do not train twice a week; they come three days running and then not for a fortnight.

And the loss is concentrated in one step. Across 1,785 first-timers, 40.5% make a second visit, and of those 70% go on to a third. 59.5 points of the cohort are lost at the first repeat visit — and then the curve reverses: from the second visit onward, each next visit is more likely than the last.

The single sentence version

This studio does not leak members steadily. It loses most of them once, in their first fortnight, and it never sees the moment coming because it measures attendance rather than the interval between visits.

When members come

Mornings carry the studio. The evening block carries a smaller group of equally committed members, on the same timetable.

September recorded 1,404 check-ins across 394 members. Read by the hour, the pattern is emphatic: 252 check-ins at 09:00 and 265 at 11:00, against 226 at 18:00 and 223 at 19:00. Read by the member rather than the visit, the same picture hardens — 249 of the 394 members have their modal hour before noon, 129 after five, and 16 in between.

The two groups are the same customers at different times of day. Morning-first members averaged 3.72 visits in the month, evening-first members 3.57 and midday members 1.06 — within a few per cent of each other. The evening block is not a lesser audience; it is simply a smaller one, and it is scheduled as though it were the larger.

How often members come

The median interval between two visits by the same person is one day. Nobody designs a timetable for that.

Look at the interval between one visit and the next for the same member, across the whole ledger, and the studio’s real rhythm appears — and it is not weekly. 17,662 of 34,937 consecutive-visit gaps are a day or less, and 85% are within a week. The median gap is 1 day.

Members come in bursts: several sessions close together, then a pause. That pattern is usually read as inconsistency, and it is usually treated with plans and targets that assume a weekly rhythm the members have never had. The commercially important consequence is different and more useful: a pause is normal. The studio cannot tell a healthy gap from a lapse by the gap alone, and should not try — what matters is whether the member comes back, and how fast.

How often members buy

Purchases cluster even more tightly than visits, and the buying decision is made in the first fortnight.

Across every repeat purchase on record, the median gap between two purchases by the same person is 14 days, and 64% of all repeat purchases happen within thirty days. The gaps then thin sharply: 668 run to two months, 315 to three, and 409 run to six months.

Two thirds of Bandra’s buyers have made exactly one purchase ever — 53.5% of the file. For the rest, buying is a monthly act, not an annual one. That leads to a simple operational conclusion: the renewal offer belongs in the first fortnight of a membership, while the member is at peak usage, rather than in its final week when the visits have already stopped.

The rhythm of the month

The desk is busiest in the hour after the morning classes, and the month is busiest in its first fortnight.

Sales are recorded at the moment they are taken, and the timestamps are unambiguous. The single busiest hour of the 2026 trading year is 12:00 — after the 09:00 and 11:00 classes come out, not before them. The busiest weekday for sales is Saturday and the quietest Sunday. Within the month there are two peaks, around the 11th to the 14th and again at the very end, with a clear trough in the last full week.

Put together, the studio’s own transaction log says where a sales push should sit: at the desk, at noon, in the first half of the month. That is not a marketing insight so much as a scheduling one — but it is the difference between an offer that arrives during a member’s routine and one that arrives after it.

The shape of the base

52% of members came once. The most frequent tenth delivered 42% of the attendance.

September’s 394 members do not distribute like a membership base. 205 of them came exactly once — 52% of the base — while the 39 members who came most often delivered 42% of every seat filled. The middle of the distribution is thinner than the extremes.

This is the arithmetic behind the studio’s headline numbers. A base that is 52% single-visit will show reasonable member counts and weak attendance in the same month, every month, until the single-visit group is either converted or discounted. The 39 regulars are not a bonus; they are the studio.

Frequency through the year

September 2026 is the lowest full month for visits per member since the series began.

Attendance is a product of two numbers — how many members and how often they come — and the studio manages the first far more actively than the second. Across the last eighteen months, visits per member have run between 2.29 and 4.92. September 2025 sat at 4.84; September 2026 came in at 3.56.

The member count over the same period has generally risen, which is why the fall in frequency has been easy to miss: more members, each coming less often, produces a member-count headline that looks healthy and an attendance number that is flat to falling. Both numbers are true, and only one of them explains the other.

Routine and variety

Members who do one thing repeatedly stay longer than members who do many things once — and the best outcome is both.

38% of September’s members used more than one format in the month, and 94 members (24% of the base) made three or more visits to a single format. The two behaviours are not in tension. A member who has a home format and also explores is the member who is still here next September; a member who does neither is the member who came once.

The check-in ledger already records each member’s modal hour and favourite format. Nothing in the studio currently uses either one to hold a slot, invite a member into the next format, or notice when a routine breaks. That is the cheapest personalisation available to Bandra, and it needs no new system.

Recommendations

Twelve actions, ordered by the size of the behaviour they change
01

Own the second visit as a product, not an outcome

Studio Manager + Front Desk

ReasoningAcross 1,785 Bandra first-timers, 40.5% reach a second visit and 28.5% reach a third. The first repeat visit is the gate: 59.5 points of the cohort never come back a second time.

Expected impactBut the gate is also the only one. Of the members who return once, 70% return again; of those, 82% come back a fourth time. Bandra does not lose members steadily — it loses them once, at the first repeat visit, and almost everyone who clears that step stays. An owned, dated, booked second class is the whole of the retention strategy.

02

Build the timetable around mornings, because that is when members come

Programming + Studio Manager

Reasoning249 of the 394 members who trained in September do most of their training before noon, against 129 who come in the evening and 16 at midday. Morning-first members averaged 3.72 visits in the month and evening-first members 3.57.

Expected impactThe studio schedules its evenings as heavily as its mornings on the assumption that working members come after work. The member data says its audience is a morning audience that also uses the evening. Rebalancing a third of the evening timetable into the 09:00 and 11:00 blocks follows the members rather than fighting them.

03

Stop expecting members to come on a schedule, and design for the cluster

Studio Manager

ReasoningThe median gap between a member’s visits is 1 day. 85% of every gap on record is a week or less, and more than half are within a day. Members do not train twice a week on a rhythm; they come in bursts.

Expected impactA studio that believes in the rhythm sells rigid plans and measures consistency. A studio that understands the cluster sells flexibility and measures return rate. The same member behaviour reads as churn under a rhythm and as normal under a cluster — and the second reading is the correct one.

04

Sell the next purchase at the point of the last one

Front Desk + CRM

ReasoningThe median gap between a member’s purchases is 14 days, and 64% of all repeat purchases happen inside a month. 668 gaps run to two months and 409 run to half a year.

Expected impactBuying behaviour at this studio is monthly, which means the renewal conversation belongs in the first fortnight of the membership rather than in its final week. A renewal offered when a member is at peak usage renews far more often than one offered after the visits have already stopped.

05

Treat the single-visit member as the studio’s core problem

Front Desk + CRM

Reasoning205 of 394 members — 52% — came exactly once in September. At the other end, the 39 most frequent members delivered 42% of every seat the studio filled.

Expected impactA base where a third of the people come once and a tenth deliver a third of the attendance is not a membership base; it is a trial pool with a small permanent core. The economics of the studio are decided at the join between those two groups, and that join is the second visit.

06

Give every member a preferred slot, and keep it

Programming + CRM

Reasoning94 members — 24% of the base — made three or more visits to the same format in September, and 38% of members used two or more formats in the month.

Expected impactA member with a standing slot is a member with a habit, and a habit is far harder to break than a preference. The studio can see each member’s modal hour and favourite format from its own check-in ledger; nothing currently uses either to hold the slot for them.

07

Read the monthly frequency number as the studio’s headline metric

Management

ReasoningVisits per member have run between 2.29 and 4.92 across the last eighteen months, and September 2026’s 3.56 is the lowest full month in the series. Attendance, revenue and member counts are all downstream of it.

Expected impactThis is the only number on the studio’s dashboard that moves before the others and explains all of them. A base that grows while frequency falls will produce flat attendance and a falling revenue-per-member, which is precisely what the last quarter has looked like.

08

Put the members who came once into a fourteen-day sequence

CRM + Front Desk

Reasoning205 members came once in September and have not been seen since. The relative-second-visit data says most of them will not come back on their own, and the ones who do will arrive within a week of their first class.

Expected impactA fortnight is the entire window. A message on day two, a booked class on day five and a call on day ten will recover more of this group than any offer made to the whole base, because it arrives while the first class is still the member’s most recent memory of the studio.

09

Schedule the money, not just the classes

Front Desk + Finance

ReasoningSales peak at 12:00 — after the morning classes rather than before them — and 18% of the year’s September-to-date sales fall in the first week of the month against 31% in the last week.

Expected impactThe desk needs staffing, offers and renewal conversations where the money already is. Placing the sales push in the hour after the morning block, and in the first fortnight of the month, works with the members’ own behaviour rather than against it.

10

Give the quiet regular a reason to come back this week

CRM + Studio Manager

ReasoningThe members who come most often carry the studio, and they are the ones whose absence costs the most. A member who drops from ten visits a month to five is invisible in every headline attendance figure and is worth more than a new joiner.

Expected impactA weekly list of members whose visits have fallen by half against their own four-week average — not against the studio’s — is the highest-yield retention tool available, and it needs no new data collection. The studio measures everyone against the same target; habit should be measured against the member’s own baseline.

11

Measure the base by how often it comes, not only by how many it is

Management

ReasoningSeptember had 394 members and 1,404 visits. September 2025 had fewer members and more visits. A member-count headline reads as growth in a month whose attendance fell.

Expected impactThe base and its frequency need to be reported side by side in every monthly pack, because they can move in opposite directions and one of them flatters. The studio has spent this year growing the numerator of a ratio whose denominator was shrinking.

12

Keep the two habits that are working

Studio Manager

Reasoning38% of September’s members used more than one format, and 24% made three or more visits to a single format. Both behaviours are associated with longer membership lives than either alone.

Expected impactVariety and routine are not opposites here; they are the two halves of a member who stays. Every programming and induction decision should be judged by whether it encourages one of them, and the studio should report both monthly.

Sources and method

Behaviour is read from the Day End Part 4 check-in ledger, filtered to Supreme HQ, Bandra (39,777 attended entries across 877 days). A member is counted in a month if they checked in at least once; revenue-weighted activity is deliberately excluded so that one large purchase cannot make a quiet member look active.
Buying behaviour comes from the raw sales export, filtered to Bandra and to records with a valid sale date. Consecutive-visit and consecutive-purchase gaps are computed within each person, so each member contributes at most one observation per pair.
The visit-number curve uses every member whose first visit was in the year to August 2025, so that every cohort member has had at least twelve months to accumulate visits. Members who joined later are excluded rather than truncated.
Cash figures are as recorded at snapshot on 18 October 2026 and are gross, not contribution margin.
