Report six of seven · Booking behaviour and price

Late cancellations and discounts

Two ways a studio gives away value: a seat booked and released too late, and a price quietly marked down. In September, Bandra released 199 seats late. In 2026 it has given away ₹25,57,453 in discounts at a depth of 13.2% — the deepest it has ever discounted. This report measures both, and separates the part of each that is a real problem from the part that is simply the cost of running a studio.

Start here

Booking discipline has genuinely improved. Discounting has genuinely worsened. Both are choices, and only one is being managed.

Start with the good news, because it is real and it is measurable. Across every seat booked at Bandra this year, 86.3% were attended and only 12.9% became a late cancellation. In 2025 those figures were 72.8% and 27.1%; in 2024, 80.4% and 19.2%. Late cancellations fell from 6,660 seats in 2025 to 2,064 this year, while the number of attending visits rose. Whatever changed in how the studio communicates expections around bookings, it worked.

In September 2026 specifically, 199 seats were released late against 1,404 attendances. That is 14.2 late cancellations for every hundred classes attended — a level the studio can live with, and one it can reduce further.

The discount story moves the other way. The studio discounted ₹25,57,453 in 2026 so far, at a depth of 13.2% of gross, against 8.0% in 2024. Discount spread has fallen — fewer sales carry an offer — but the offers that are made are deeper, and they are concentrated on the products that needed them least. That is the finding worth acting on.

What a released seat costs

A late cancellation is a seat the studio paid for and did not use. Whether that matters depends entirely on how full the room was.

The value of a released seat is not fixed. It is worth almost nothing in a half-empty room and a great deal in a full one. In September the board ran at 48.7% occupancy on the booking book when those 199 seats were released, against 60.5% in September 2025. More than half the seats were unsold at the moment they were given back.

That changes the remedy. Charging for a late cancellation recovers a little; reselling the seat recovers the whole ticket and keeps the member. And the seats are not evenly distributed: 64 of them were PowerCycle, 52 FIT and 39 Barre 57, so the recovery problem is concentrated in the three formats that between them carry most of the week.

Who cancels, and when

Cancelling is concentrated, and it is concentrated among the studio’s most engaged members.

In September 110 members released a booked seat late. But 68 of them did it once and 29 did it three times or more, and the ten heaviest accounts for 23.6% of every cancellation in the month. Across the entire paid membership book the concentration is starker still: the worst tenth of memberships account for 80.3% of all 10,104 late cancellations ever recorded.

The timing says something the studio can use. Cancellations cluster at 9:00, 11:00, 18:00, the mid-morning block, and again in the early evening — the two windows where the studio’s paying demand is strongest. By day, Thursday and Saturday carry the most. These are not random acts of forgetfulness; they are the hours where a working member’s day most often gets in the way.

What cancelling actually predicts

It does not predict leaving. It predicts a member whose week has stopped fitting — which is more useful.

Sort the 3,974 paid memberships by how often they cancelled and a clear picture emerges. Members who never cancelled completed an average of 2.3 classes. Those who cancelled more than half of everything they booked completed 10.0 and averaged 10.8 late cancellations each. Between those two extremes, engagement rises before it falls: the members who cancel a little attend more than the members who never cancel at all.

And yet the renewal rate barely moves across the bands — 6.6% to 13.3%. Cancelling is a symptom, not a cause. That is precisely why it is valuable: it appears weeks before the member stops coming, it is already recorded, and nobody reads it.

The discount book

The studio is giving away more per sale than ever, to fewer sales, and mostly on the products that did not need it.

Across the years on file Bandra has given away ₹55,02,355 of discount against ₹5,32,44,556 of gross sales. The trend in depth is unmistakable: 8.0% in 2024, 9.0% in 2025, 13.2% in 2026. At the same time the spread narrowed — 24% of sales carried a discount in 2024 against 18% this year. The studio has stopped discounting casually and started discounting decisively, which is a different behaviour, not obviously a better one.

Where the money goes is the more interesting question. In 2026 the 1 Month Unlimited carried 21.6% depth across 68% of its sales and the 10 Class Package was discounted on 95% of its sales, while the 12 Class Package sat at 6.4% and the single-class products at almost nothing at all.

Do discounts work?

Pooled, they look like the best investment the studio makes. Controlled for what was bought, the effect disappears — and in places reverses.

Take every first-time buyer whose first purchase is at least six months old and split them by whether that first purchase carried a discount. Pooled, the answer is spectacular: discounted first-buyers repeat at 67.4% and are worth ₹62,483 in lifetime value, against 44.5% and ₹17,841 for those who paid full price.

That comparison is not fair, because the products that carry discounts are not the products that carry full price. Hold the product constant and the picture changes:

12 Class Package — discounted first-buyers repeat at 77.3% against 94.0% for full-price buyers, with lifetime value of ₹54,975 against ₹1,02,470.
1 Month Unlimited — discounted first-buyers repeat at 83.7% against 91.2% for full-price buyers, with lifetime value of ₹80,308 against ₹1,07,053.

The pooled advantage was composition. Members who happened to start on an unlimited or package product were always going to stay longer, and those products happen to carry the offers. On this evidence the studio has no proof that its discounts buy loyalty — only that they reduce the price of sales it would largely have made anyway. That is a testable proposition, and it is not currently being tested.

Recommendations

Nine actions, ordered by the size of the value each protects
01

Put a late-cancellation charge against the seat, and enforce it

Front Desk + Studio Manager

Reasoning2,064 seats were released late in 2026 at Bandra, and the studio recovered nothing for them. The already-improving trend — from 27.1% of the book in 2025 to 12.9% this year — shows members respond to being held to the booking.

Expected impactA late cancellation at an average of ₹797 a seat is worth real money. Charging or crediting a fraction of it, and applying the rule to everyone, is the single cheapest revenue line available and it costs the studio nothing to introduce.

02

Offer the released seat back to the waitlist, automatically

CRM + Front Desk

ReasoningSeptember’s 199 late cancellations were released at an average of 48.7% board occupancy — a third of the board was still unsold when they were given back. 64 of them were PowerCycle, 52 were FIT.

Expected impactA seat released four hours before the class is worth almost nothing to the studio and a great deal to a member who could not book. Auto-offering released seats to the waitlist and to members who have not trained that week converts a loss into a visit at no cost.

03

Contact the members who cancel repeatedly, personally

CRM

Reasoning110 members cancelled in September, but 29 members made three or more cancellations and the top ten made 23.6% of all of them. Across the paid book, the worst 10% of memberships carry 80.3% of every late cancellation ever recorded.

Expected impactRepeat cancelling is not laziness, it is a schedule that does not fit. These are engaged members whose booking and life are out of step. A short call about which slots actually work for them is cheaper than a discount and more likely to hold them.

04

Use the cancellation signal as an early-warning field

CRM + Studio Manager

ReasoningAcross 3,974 paid memberships, members who never cancelled completed 2.3 classes; those who cancelled more than half completed 10.0 and averaged 10.8 late cancellations each. Renewal rates barely move across the bands.

Expected impactCancelling is a symptom of a life getting busier, not a cause of leaving — which is exactly why it is worth acting on the first time it appears, not after the membership ends. A member whose cancellation rate crosses a threshold should enter a contact sequence the same week.

05

Re-time the classes that lose their bookings

Programming

ReasoningCancellations cluster at 9:00, 11:00, 18:00, and by day on Thursday, Saturday, Tuesday. The formats that lose the most seats are PowerCycle, FIT, Barre 57.

Expected impactA cancellation is a member telling the studio that the time does not work. Where a slot loses a fifth or more of its bookings every week, moving the slot or shortening the format is a programming fix rather than a compliance problem.

06

Stop discounting the products that renew best

Marketing + Studio Manager

ReasoningThe 1 Month Unlimited carries 21.6% discount depth and 68% of its sales are discounted. The 3 Month Unlimited carries 18.1%, and the 10 Class Package is discounted on 95% of its sales.

Expected impactThe studio is buying demand for the products that were already going to sell. Every rupee given away on a 1 Month Unlimited is a rupee taken off a product whose lifetime value is the highest in the range.

07

Test the discount on the products where it has never been tried

Marketing

ReasoningThe 8 Class Package carries 1.7% depth on 13% of sales and the 12 Class Package 6.4% on 54%, while the 1 Month Unlimited sits at 21.6%.

Expected impactThe pricing energy is concentrated in one product. A controlled trial of a modest offer on a package product, measured on renewal rather than on units sold, would tell the studio whether its discounts work at all before it signs another year of them.

08

Set a discount ceiling and report depth by seller

Finance + Management

ReasoningSellers with twenty or more 2026 sales range from 8.3% to 17.2% in discount depth, and the number of discounted sales ranges from 14% to 28% of their book. Nobody is doing anything wrong; nobody is being told the range exists.

Expected impactPublishing the range monthly, without naming individuals, moves behaviour faster than any policy. A ceiling with an approval route above it turns discounting from a habit into a decision.

09

Price the discount honestly in the P&L

Finance

ReasoningAcross the years on file the studio has given away ₹55,02,355 against ₹5,32,44,556 of gross sales, at an average depth of 10.3% — and 2026 is the deepest year on record at 13.2%.

Expected impactDiscount is a cost of sale and belongs on the same line as marketing. Reported together, the studio can see what it spent to acquire demand in cash versus in price, and can compare the two for the first time.

10

Never discount the first month — discount the second

Marketing + Finance

ReasoningOn the 12 Class Package, first-buyers who paid full price repeat at 28.6% and are worth ₹25,385. First-buyers who were discounted repeat at 28.4% and are worth ₹23,471. The same pattern holds on the 1 Month Unlimited — 14.3% against 46.8% repeat, at ₹23,347 against ₹35,476.

Expected impactThe offer is not the problem; its timing is. A discount on the first purchase lowers the value of the members the studio attracts at the exact moment it is trying to establish a habit at full commitment. Moving the same money to the first renewal — a retention offer rather than an acquisition offer — rewards the behaviour the studio actually wants and stops setting the price expectation at the beginning of the relationship.

11

Publish late-cancellation rate by trainer as a coaching number

Studio Manager

ReasoningAcross trainers with a hundred or more September bookings the late-cancellation rate runs from 6.3% to 18.3% — a four-fold spread on the same booking policy. Anisha Shah runs 18.3% and Reshma Sharma 6.3%.

Expected impactA released seat is a member deciding, at short notice, that the hour no longer fits. When the rate is high for one trainer and low for another teaching the same policy, the difference is usually the slot rather than the person. Read as a coaching number rather than a compliance one, it is the earliest signal the studio has that a slot is drifting out of its members’ lives.

12

Show the member what the discount was worth

Marketing + Front Desk

ReasoningThe studio gave away ₹25,57,453 in 2026 alone, at an average depth of 13.2% of gross. Across the years on file the figure is ₹55,02,355 across every year on file.

Expected impactA discount that arrives silently as a lower price is read as the price. A discount that arrives as a line on the receipt, with a value against it, is read as a favour — and favours are repaid. Showing the saving costs nothing, changes no policy, and is the only part of the discount programme the member ever sees.
