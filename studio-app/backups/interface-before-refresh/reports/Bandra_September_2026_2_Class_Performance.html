Start here

The classes got better. There are simply fewer good ones than there should be.

Class performance is the one part of Bandra’s September that improved on nearly every measure, and it deserves to be said clearly before the problems. The studio ran 259 classes against 273 a year earlier — a leaner timetable — and filled 42.6% of the seats against 41.8%. Revenue attributed to attended classes rose from ₹9,81,453 to ₹13,06,451, up 33%, making it the only revenue line in the studio that grew. Revenue per class rose 40% to ₹5,044; revenue per attendee rose from ₹665 to ₹931. Late cancellations fell from 658 to 199.

Every one of those is a genuine operational gain, and two Septembers of improvement in a row — from 27.3% in 2024 to 41.8% to 42.6% — is a trend rather than noise.

What the same numbers also show is a large and specific inefficiency that has been carried for three years without being addressed. The studio holds a fixed-cost timetable and fills just over two seats in five. In September 2026 it offered 3,293 seats and used 1,404 of them. The unused 1,889 seats are not evenly spread: they are concentrated in particular formats, particular days and particular hours, and the concentration is stable enough across two years to be treated as a design flaw rather than a bad month.

The single most useful sentence in this report is this: Studio PowerCycle runs 35% of the timetable and fills 35.0% of it, while Studio FIT runs 15% of the timetable and fills 71.0%. Both are good products with loyal followings. Only one of them is being given the right amount of the week.

And a fourth finding, which is new in this edition and changes what the first three mean. The studio is not short of people. 394 members trained here in September 2026 against 305 a year earlier — a wider base than in either of the last two Septembers. What fell was how often they came: 3.56 visits each against 4.84. The 78 members who were here in both Septembers cut their own frequency 35%, and those missing 252 visits are 18% of everything the studio filled this month. The room is not emptying because people stopped coming. It is emptying because the people who come are coming less.

The loyal core halved its frequency

Seventy-eight people who trained here last September came back this September. Between them they gave the studio 252 fewer visits.

78 members trained at Bandra in both September 2025 and September 2026. This is a true like-for-like group — same people, same month, twelve months apart. In September 2025 they made 713 visits (9.14 each). In September 2026 they made 461 (5.91 each). That is a fall of 35.3% in the frequency of the studio’s most committed customers.

The arithmetic matters more than the percentage. Those 252 lost visits are 18% of every seat that was filled in September 2026. Had the core held its year-ago frequency, September 2026 would have recorded 1,656 attendances — ahead of September 2025’s 1,476, not behind it. The month’s decline is not an acquisition failure at all. It is a frequency failure among people who already know the studio.

Nobody was lost. Nothing was converted.

The churn is almost entirely people who came once and never came back — and it is being replaced in volume.

Of the 227 members who trained in September 2025 and did not appear in September 2026, 126 had made exactly one visit in that month, and 105 of the 227 were last seen in September 2025 itself. On lifetime numbers the distinction is stark: the members who stayed had averaged 161 career visits, while the ones who left averaged 34. The studio is not losing its regulars. It is failing to convert its trials.

And the replacements are working harder than the departures did. The 316 members who had no September 2025 visit delivered 943 attendances this month; the 227 who left behind them had delivered only 763 a year ago. New blood out-performed old losses by 24%. The problem is not that the studio cannot recruit. It is that it recruits at the top of the funnel and leaks at the bottom.

What the booking book says

Bookings fell four times faster than attendance. That is the cleanest warning in the data.

Attendance is a lagging number — it tells you what happened in the room. The booking book tells you what members intended to do, and it moved sharply. Members booked 2,134 seats in September 2025 and 1,604 in September 2026, a fall of 25%. Attendance fell only 4.9%. The two numbers have come apart, and the gap is entirely explained by discipline: 658 late cancellations became 199.

Two things are true at once. The studio is now far better at converting an intention into a visit — 69.2% of booked seats were actually used a year ago, against 87.5% now, and revenue per booked seat rose from ₹460 to ₹814. But the intention pool itself is drying up: the book now covers only 48.7% of the seats on the board, down from 60.5%. Tightening cancellation policy has improved the conversion of demand that already exists. It has not created any new demand.

Why this matters more than the fill rate

Fill rate is computed after the fact. Bookings are the leading indicator, and the book is now half-empty 48.7% of capacity was ever claimed. A timetable designed for 3,293 seat-slots is being asked to serve a book of 1,604. Until the booking rate rises, no amount of scheduling refinement will fill the room — there is nothing left to convert.

The room is bigger than the demand

Small rooms fill. Large rooms do not. The studio has been quietly fixing this — and has further to go.

Sort the month’s sessions by how many seats the room holds and a clean pattern appears. Rooms of ten and eleven seats ran 62.4% full across 62 sessions. Rooms of twelve and thirteen ran 37.3% across 99 sessions, and rooms of fourteen or more ran 38.5% across 98. The same pattern held a year earlier, so this is a property of the timetable, not of September.

The encouraging part is that the studio has already begun to move. September 2025 ran 49 sessions in ten-to-eleven-seat rooms; September 2026 ran 62. Sessions in the largest rooms fell from 122 to 98. Shifting a class into a smaller room does not change how many people come — it changes how full the room looks and how much the same demand is worth, and revenue per session in the small-room band was ₹6,385 against ₹4,622 elsewhere.

16 per cent of the room was free

Complimentary seats rose by more than half while paid attendance fell. Barre 57 is the most affected.

Of the 1,404 attendances recorded in September 2026, 225 carried no payment — 16.0% of the room, against 9.7% a year earlier. Complimentary seats rose 57% while paid seats fell. Comps are not wrong in themselves: staff visits, partner classes and make-goods are legitimate. But they are invisible in an attendance-only report, and at this scale they change the meaning of the fill rate. A 42.6% fill on 3,293 seats is 1,404 attendances; 225 of them produced no revenue at all.

Studio Barre 57 is the format most affected: 27 of its 331 attendances (27%) were complimentary, against 4.0% for Studio FIT. The two hosted classes in the month — the Physique 57 partner sessions on 13 and 23 September — were 100% complimentary, 24 seats, zero revenue, and are correctly recorded as marketing rather than trading.

Who a first class brings in — and whether they come back

The day, hour and format of somebody’s very first class predicts their lifetime with unusual accuracy.

Take every member whose first ever Bandra class fell from January 2025 onward, follow them for their whole history, and split by the circumstances of that first visit. The differences are large enough to schedule against.

By day: people whose first class was on a Tuesday reach a third visit 32.0% of the time; those who started on a Sunday, 14.6%. By hour: a first class before 9am converts to a third visit 27.8% of the time and yields 9.5 visits on average, against 15.9% and 5.0 visits for an afternoon start. By format: a first class in Studio Barre 57 leads to a third visit 28.3% of the time and 8.4 visits, while a first class in Studio Mat 57 leads to 11.6% and 2.5 visits.

None of this means a Tuesday class is better than a Sunday class. It means that the people willing to start on a Tuesday, or before work, or in the Barre room, are a different kind of customer. The studio currently gives trials and first-timers to whichever slot has room. The data says that decision has a measurable twelve-month consequence, and it is currently being made by default rather than by design.

Three formats tell the story.

Studio FIT is the studio’s best asset. It fills 71.0% of its seats, averages 7.1 people a class, earns ₹6,999 per class — the highest of any format — and drew 63 more attendees than last September while adding 10 sessions. It has zero empty classes. It is also the format with the lowest share of intro-offer attendance, meaning its audience is committed members rather than trials.

Studio PowerCycle is the studio’s biggest format and its least efficient. It runs 90 sessions, more than a third of the month, and fills 35.0% — a fall of 8.8 points year on year, and the only major decline in the table. It absorbed 48 fewer attendees. It also carries the studio’s trial programme: 8% of its attendance arrives on an intro offer. PowerCycle is doing two jobs — a flagship product and a trial venue — and the second is crowding out the first.

Studio Barre 57 is the quiet improver. Utilisation rose +9.7 points to 44.6% on 15 fewer sessions, with attendance essentially flat. The studio cut Barre capacity and the format got tighter as a result — which is direct evidence that the same approach would work elsewhere.

Two formats are chronic underperformers. Studio Cardio Barre filled 27.6%, almost identical to its 27.2% last year, on 21 sessions. Studio Recovery filled 29.8%, down 17.2 points. Together they account for 27 sessions and 106 attendances. Neither is a bad class; both are occupying slots that the studio’s strongest formats could use.

Utilisation falls from 47.7% in the first week of September to 41.8% in the fourth, while the number of sessions run falls only slightly. September 2025 followed the same shape, from 51.5% to 34.3%. Two consecutive years of the same arc means this is not an event, a festival or a marketing failure — it is the studio’s booking rhythm.

The likely mechanism is straightforward: members front-load their bookings when a new month or a new package cycle begins, and the final week runs on residual commitment. Whatever the cause, the shape is predictable, and a predictable dip is a schedulable one.

Read alongside the utilisation figures, that pairing explains the whole timetable problem. Recommendations

Fourteen actions, ordered by expected value

The first four are timetable decisions that cost nothing and can be made before next month’s schedule is published. The rest build the measurement and conversion discipline that would keep the improvements.

01

Right-size Studio PowerCycle

Programming + Studio Manager

ReasoningPowerCycle runs 90 sessions — 35% of the entire timetable — and fills 35.0% of its seats, down 8.8 points on last September. It is the only major format whose utilisation fell, and it absorbed 48 fewer attendees than a year ago. Meanwhile its capacity per class is 13.0 seats against an average attendance of 4.56.

Expected impactCutting PowerCycle’s average class size from 13 to 10 seats frees roughly 270 seat-hours a month without touching the timetable, and removes 8 empty sessions from the board. Raising its fill to Studio FIT’s 71% would add roughly 421 attendances a month.

02

Make Studio FIT the template, then scale it

Programming

ReasoningStudio FIT is the studio’s best-performing format by a distance: 71.0% utilisation on 39 sessions, an average class of 7.1, +63 more attendees than last September, and the highest revenue per class of any format at ₹6,999. It achieved this while adding 10 sessions. Only 1% of its attendance is on intro offers — these are committed members.

Expected impactAdding 3 FIT sessions a week at current fill adds about 91 attendances a month, worth roughly ₹89,669 of class revenue, using capacity the studio already owns.

03

Close the early-morning black hole

Studio Manager + Programming

ReasoningSessions starting before 09:00 fill 31.3% — 19 points below the late-morning peak — and account for 55 of the month’s sessions. Eight of the eleven fully empty sessions in September started at 07:30 or 09:15, and every one of them was PowerCycle.

Expected impactConsolidating the weakest early slots into the 09:00–11:00 window, or converting them to a format with proven early demand, would remove about 13 unproductive sessions a month and lift overall utilisation by an estimated 2–3 points.

04

Rebalance the week away from Wednesday

Programming

ReasoningWednesday fills 30.9% against Saturday’s 56.2% — a 25-point gap inside the same week. Wednesday also runs 51 sessions, more than any day except Tuesday, and earns the lowest revenue per class at ₹3,887 against Saturday’s ₹6,152.

Expected impactMoving 4 of Wednesday’s weakest sessions to Saturday and Sunday mornings, where utilisation is 56% and 48%, redirects roughly 122 attendances a month into demand that already exists.

05

Fix the fourth-week fade

Programming + Marketing

ReasoningUtilisation falls from 47.7% in week one to 41.8% in week four while the number of sessions run stays broadly constant. September 2025 showed the identical pattern, which means this is a structural rhythm rather than a September event.

Expected impactHolding week four to the week-one rate would add roughly 40 attendances in a single week. A mid-month re-engagement push aimed at members who have not booked in 14 days is the natural instrument.

06

Stop scheduling classes that draw two people

Programming

Reasoning49 of 259 sessions (19%) ran to two attendees or fewer, including 11 that were completely empty. Those sessions tied up 643 seats on the schedule. PowerCycle accounts for 26 of them.

Expected impactConsolidating the leanest 29 of these sessions would free roughly 385 seat-hours a month and give the studio a timetable it can promote honestly. Every empty class also carries a trainer cost that this data does not capture.

07

Build the conversion pathway that the hosted classes lack

Marketing + Studio Manager

ReasoningAll 3 hosted and partner classes in September ran at 83% utilisation — the fullest classes of the month — and produced ₹0 of class revenue, because every attendee was complimentary. Separately, hosted-class leads convert to membership at 8.3% against 24.2% for referrals.

Expected impactThese are the studio’s best-attended sessions and its worst-converting source. Attaching a booked second class to every hosted attendee at the point of arrival is the cheapest fix available and would move the modelled value of the programme materially without changing what it costs to run.

08

Publish utilisation, not just attendance

Studio Manager

ReasoningThe studio tracks attendance well but the timetable is set on judgement. This September a 25-point utilisation spread existed between two days, a 19-point spread existed between two dayparts, and 6 of 12 formats filled less than 40% of their seats. None of that is visible in an attendance-only report, because attendance is a count and utilisation is a ratio.

Expected impactOne monthly utilisation table, read before the next month’s timetable is built, changes the basis of every scheduling decision from "did anyone come" to "was this the right room".

09

Shrink the room before shrinking the timetable

Studio Manager + Programming

ReasoningRooms of ten to eleven seats ran 62.4% full across 62 sessions; rooms of twelve to thirteen ran 37.3% across 99 sessions, and rooms of fourteen or more ran 38.5% across 98. The studio has already moved in this direction — sessions in ten-to-eleven-seat rooms rose from 49 to 62 — and the effect on the fill rate is arithmetic, not heroics.

Expected impactThe 99 sessions sitting in the twelve-to-thirteen band are the largest single block of addressable inefficiency in the timetable. Capping those rooms at eleven seats removes roughly 198 seat-slots a month that nobody is buying, and lifts overall utilisation by around 6.0 points without changing a single thing about who teaches or when.

10

Put a hard line under complimentary seats

Studio Manager + Finance

ReasoningComplimentary seats rose 57% year on year, to 225 of 1404 attendances — 16.0% of the room, against 9.7% a year earlier. Studio Barre 57 alone ran 27 comp seats on 331 attendances (27%).

Expected impactComps are legitimate but they are invisible in the fill rate and the fill rate is what the timetable is judged on. A monthly comp budget by format — and a rule that a comp consumes the same seat a paying member could have bought — would make the 225 seats visible. At September’s revenue per head of ₹931 that is ₹2,09,475 of delivered value a month to account for.

11

Design the first visit, do not default it

Studio Manager + Programming

ReasoningA first class on a Tuesday leads to a third visit 32.0% of the time; on a Sunday, 14.6%. Before 9am, 27.8%; in the afternoon, 15.9%. In Studio Barre 57, 28.3%; in Studio Mat 57, 11.6%.

Expected impactTrialists are currently placed wherever there is room. Placing them deliberately — into the Tuesday and early-morning slots that convert at nearly double the afternoon rate — costs nothing and operates on the 3025 first-timers this analysis covers. Even a five-point lift in third-visit conversion is worth about ₹5,63,255 of delivered value.

12

Manage the booking book, not the attendance

Studio Manager

ReasoningBookings fell 25% against a 4.9% fall in attendance, and the book now covers only 48.7% of the seats on the board, down from 60.5%. Attendance told the studio after the fact that September was quieter; the booking book said so in advance.

Expected impactA weekly booked-versus-capacity ratio for the coming fortnight, read alongside the fill rate for the past one, converts the timetable from a rear-view report into a leading one. It is the difference between noticing the current quarter and managing the next one.

13

Report frequency per active member, not attendance alone

Studio Manager + Management

ReasoningThe member base is widening while volume falls: 394 members trained this September against 305 last, but each of them came 3.56 times against 4.84. Attendance is a lagging number; visits per active member is the number that moves first.

Expected impactA monthly line for visits per member, sitting beside the member count, would have flagged this decline in the spring instead of at the September review. It costs the studio nothing to produce and it changes what the studio notices.

14

Build a continuity tier from the members who never leave

Studio Manager + CRM

ReasoningOnly 18 members trained at Bandra in all three of September 2024, 2025 and 2026. Against a base of 394 members this September, that is 4.6% who have kept the habit across three years of a business that has run 355 and 305 members in the same months.

Expected impactThese 18 people are the studio’s only proof that a member can stay. They should be known by name, asked what holds them, and used as the benchmark against which every retention initiative is measured. A studio that cannot describe its longest-serving members in detail cannot design for them.

Method and limitations

How to read these numbers, and what they cannot show

Sources and method

Day End Report – Part 3 – Sessions (25,675 rows, exported 11 October 2026) is the primary source. Each row is one class occurrence at one location, with capacity, checked-in, booked, late-cancelled, complimentary, revenue and a breakdown of how attendees paid. Day End Report – Part 3 – Teacher Recurring (3,294 rows) is the same data aggregated per recurring weekly slot and reconciles to the Sessions file exactly.

Utilisation is computed here as checked-in attendances divided by capacity offered, per class occurrence. This is the studio’s own definition and it is the one used throughout. It differs from a count of bookings, and it counts a seat as unused whether the member never booked it or booked and did not turn up.

A note on the recurring-slot file

Two columns in the Teacher Recurring export are mislabelled in the platform: the column named FillRate actually contains revenue per class, and the column named ClassAvgExclEmpty actually contains the fill rate. This report uses only the raw counts from that file — classes, capacity and checked-in — and recomputes every ratio itself.

Known limitations

  • No trainer cost. This data contains no payroll or cost-per-class information, so an empty class can be described as unproductive but not quantified as a loss. Part 1 of the Payroll export supplies teacher economics, and it runs only to August 2026.
  • Revenue attribution. Class revenue is the platform’s allocation of member spend to attended classes. It reconciles to 85% of September cash. It is a sound basis for comparing formats, not an accounting statement.
  • Two sessions of 20:00 and one of 16:00 are too few to draw conclusions from; hour-level figures with fewer than five sessions are marked accordingly in the workbook.
  • September 2025 followed Ganesh Chaturthi (27 August) while September 2026 absorbed it (14 September). Week-by-week comparisons near the festival are affected.
