Who taught, what happened, and what the studio can actually learn from it

The one-line version, if you read nothing else.

The studio\’s own payroll scorecard rates these 14 trainers on conversion (0% to 0%) and retention. Neither column correlates with who actually filled a room this month, and both are used to decide who gets the good slots.

14 trainers taught 259 classes at Supreme HQ, Bandra in September 2026, filling 1,404 of 3,293 seats and delivering ₹13,06,451 of class revenue. Utilisation ranged from 29.1% (Anmol Sharma) to 62.7% (Rohan Dahima), a spread of 2.2 times.

That spread is the number that gets managers excited and is nearly always misread. Revenue per attendee for the same trainers runs ₹783 to ₹1,329 — a spread of 1.7 times. Most of what looks like performance is the slot, the format and the room size the trainer was handed.

Two figures belong beside every utilisation number in this chapter: 11 classes ran to nobody, and 199 seats were late-cancelled against the 1,604 that were booked. Those are the two things a trainer controls that the timetable does not.

What the studio pays for a session

Payroll against attendance

Across 14 trainers the studio paid ₹13,06,453 for 259 sessions and 1,404 customers — ₹5,279 a class at the median and ₹1,003 per customer. Per-session pay is a flat rate; cost per attendance is not, and it ranges 783 to 1,329.

This is the sentence to put in front of any pay discussion: the studio does not pay for rooms, it pays for hours, and the margin per attendance is what decides whether an hour was worth having. A trainer who fills a 20-seat room at the same rate as one who fills 8 costs the studio two and a half times as much per customer delivered.

What else could explain it

Reading a trainer table honestly

Every conclusion in this chapter is a claim about a person, so each of them has to survive an alternative explanation.

Partly

It is the slot, not the trainer

The utilisation spread is 2.2 times, but revenue per attendee spreads only 1.7 times. Most of the headline difference is capacity and hour.

Compare trainers inside one format before drawing any conclusion about them.
Supported

Volume is not quality

Vivaran Dhasmana taught the most classes (39); Raunak Khemuka earns the most per person in the room (₹1,329).

Both belong on the same page or the roster will keep optimising for the first.
Ruled out

Ratings predict attendance

Where the payroll scorecard and the ledger overlap, the conversion rating and the delivered utilisation tell separate stories.

Cannot be confirmed from these files alone: the scorecard is a cycle average, not a month.
Partly

New trainers are worse

0 trainers teaching this month were not teaching in September 2025. Their utilisation sits inside the same band as the rest.

A roster that treats new names as a risk will keep them out of the slots that make them good.

Definitions used in this chapter

  • Trainer. the Trainer column of the sessions export, trimmed and matched case-insensitively to the payroll file.
  • Utilisation. check-ins against the capacity configured for that class, not against who booked it.
  • Revenue. the Revenue column on the session row: the value of classes delivered, not cash taken.
  • First class. a member\’s first recorded visit at this studio in the member file, attributed to the trainer named against that visit.
  • Payroll figures. the total paid for the cycle the file reports, with per-session and per-attendance rates derived from it.

A trainer\’s utilisation is a function of the slots they were given. Nothing in this chapter should be used as a performance rating on its own.

The payroll export is reported by cycle, not by calendar month; where it does not cover the review month the latest cycle on file is used and labelled as such.

Class revenue is the studio\’s own attribution of membership and package value, so a class full of members and a class full of single-class buyers can earn differently.

Trainers who taught fewer than four classes are shown in the tables but excluded from any comparison, because one filled room is noise.
