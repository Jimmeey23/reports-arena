The base is widening

More or fewer people, and what they do once they are here

The number of people who paid to train at this studio in September 2026 was 394, against 305 in September 2025 and 355 in September 2024. The base is widening.

Breadth and depth have moved in opposite directions for two years. The studio reaches +11% more people than it did two Septembers ago, while serving them 9% less often. A club that is gaining members and losing frequency is not growing; it is churning more slowly than it is recruiting.

The loyal core halved its frequency

The same people, twelve months apart

78 members trained here in both September 2025 and September 2026. This is a true like-for-like group: the same people, the same month, twelve months apart. They made 713 visits then (9.14 each) and 461 visits now (5.91 each) — 35% fewer, worth 252 attendances.

Had the core held its year-ago frequency, September 2026 would have recorded 1,656 attendances instead of 1,404. The month is not short of customers; it is short of visits from the customers it already has.

126 of the 227 people who were here last September and not this one had made exactly one visit in that month. They were never members who left; they were visitors who did not come back. No win-back campaign reaches them, because on the books they never started.

The commitment-length problem

The studio’s forward revenue cushion is thinning

Every membership that records a class count was bought on average 16.5 classes in 2026 against 74.3 in 2024 — a fall of 78% in what a member commits to at the moment of paying.

That is the whole forward-revenue argument in one column. ₹18,81,443 of cash this september-year band was collected against 752 memberships, each of which obliges the studio to deliver 16.5 classes and then ask again. Shorter commitment means more conversations per rupee, and the most fragile part of the year is the renewal the studio has to win every 16 classes.

The average member still holds 14.8 undelivered classes on the memberships bought this month. Those classes are not revenue yet; they are a promise, and the studio pays for them in room time whether or not the member turns up.

What the first purchase predicts

Intake quality, and the two-day window

What a member buys first predicts what they are worth. Across the Bandra intake file, lifetime value runs from ₹13,689 for a Single Class to ₹1,19,845 for a 3 Month Unlimited — a 8.8-fold range set by the first decision, before any coaching happens.

The same order appears in visits: 101.7 on a 3 Month Unlimited against 11.0 on a Single Class. The intro offer is therefore not a pricing question at all; it is the first programming decision the studio makes about a member.

Speed matters as much as product. Members back within a day of their first visit are worth ₹39,768 on average; those who take over a month are worth ₹13,918. Nothing in the studio currently owns the interval between visit one and visit two.

What else could explain it

The causes that are easy to miss, and the ones that are not

Every finding above has at least two possible causes. These are the candidates, and what each one would need to be true.

Partly

A quiet market

Cash moved -2.3% while the number of buyers moved -7.3%. A flat top line made of different parts is a market signal, not a sales-team signal.

Ruled out only if buyers and cash move together for three consecutive months.
Supported

The offer, not the price

Ticket size rose while the product mix shortened. Buyers are not being lost; they are being sold less commitment per sale.

Check next month whether average classes bought per membership moves.
Supported

Nobody owns the unattributed sale

a slice of the month has no salesperson attached.

A rule, not a reminder: every sale needs a name before it can be paid out.
Partly

Festival and calendar effects

Cash concentrated in week 1 / 2 / 3 / 4 / 5 shows the month trading to the payroll calendar. Public holidays and festival weeks move the same way every year and are frequently mistaken for performance.

Compare the same week-of-month pattern for three years before drawing a conclusion.

Definitions used in this chapter

  • Cash taken. the Payment Value column of the sales export, summed on Payment Date, filtered to this studio. Refunds and voids are left in as they arrive.
  • Delivered class revenue. the Revenue column of the sessions export, on classes actually taught, with at least one attendance.
  • Buyer. a distinct Member ID with at least one payment in the month. A household paying twice counts once.
  • Repeat buyer. a Member ID with two or more distinct sales in the month, not two payments on one invoice.
  • Discount depth. discount as a share of gross value; spread is the share of lines carrying any discount at all.

The sales export carries no row for a class bought at another studio and paid here, so cross-studio spend is invisible in this chapter.

Cash is not revenue. A twelve-class package sold today is twelve classes of liability; only the sessions ledger shows what has been delivered.

September 2026 is read against the same month last year rather than a rolling twelve, because attendance and pricing at this studio both move with the calendar.

Year-on-year comparisons of September are not directly comparable across years when the festival week shifts; the pattern inside the month is shown so the shift can be seen rather than assumed.
