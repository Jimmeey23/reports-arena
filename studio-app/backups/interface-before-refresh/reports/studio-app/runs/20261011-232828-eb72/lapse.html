The membership ledger for Supreme HQ, Bandra holds 8,707 rows covering 3,768 people. 3,974 of them were paid for, carrying ₹4,40,74,830 of recorded money. 78% of that money belongs to memberships now marked lapsed.

This is not a report about people who left last month. It is the whole book the studio has ever sold here, read as a funnel: what a membership is worth when it is bought, how long it lasts, how much of it is used, and how it ends. The month matters only because the month is where the studio can still act.

The median paid membership at this studio lasts 18 days. Not months. And the way it ends is not a single story: 1,107 memberships recorded no attendance at all, while the rest ran down their classes and stopped. Those are different failures, they cost different things to fix, and the studio currently treats them as one number called churn.

The two ways to lose a member

One never started. One finished.
1,107 memberships recorded no attendance at all, 13% of the ledger. 314 of them were paid for. These people bought a promise and never used it; the studio has their money and their goodwill, and it is losing both quietly.

The other failure is the opposite: a member who came, used the product, and finished it. Those memberships are not churn in any meaningful sense — they are short products selling short. The distinction decides whether the fix is an activation programme or a pricing change, and today it is not made anywhere in the reporting.

Usage tells the difference cleanly: nothing used 3,229 · up to a quarter 88 · a quarter to a half 86 · a half to three quarters 136 · three quarters to all 426 · everything or more 9. The members who used nothing have a median membership of 14 days; those who used everything ran 57 days. One group left; the other finished.

The decline that comes first

What the visit pattern says weeks before the status changes

Churn shows up in the visit pattern long before it shows up in the status. Comparing each membership’s first half against its second, across 1,734 memberships with more than three visits to split: 63% came less often in the second half, 37% by at least half, and only 26% came more often. The median run fell from 6 visits to 4.

That is the leading indicator the studio is missing. Attendance decline is visible weeks before a membership lapses, it is computable from data the studio already exports, and it identifies people who can still be reached — unlike lapsed members, whom the file says are gone.

Who is winnable

Recency, unspent value, and the ones who asked to pause

What else could explain it

Causes the studio has not considered

Churn is the chapter where the obvious explanation is usually wrong, so each candidate is stated with what would have to be true.

Supported

The product is short, not the member disloyal

The median paid membership runs 18 days and the modal product is a one-month or small-pack purchase. A short product that is fully used is not churn.

Split the churn number by whether usage exceeded 90% before deciding what to fix.
Supported

Nobody is onboarded

1,107 memberships never produced a single attendance, and 314 of those were paid for.

An activation contact in the first fourteen days is the whole intervention.
Partly

Price and discount

Discount taken at purchase shows no consistent relationship with how long a membership lives in this file.

Do not conclude discounts cause churn; conclude they do not prevent it, which is a different decision.
Ruled out

The competition took them

The exports contain no competitor or cancellation-reason field. Anything said about why members went to another studio is a hypothesis, not a finding.

Ask directly, on the day the status changes, and record the answer in the same file.

Definitions used in this chapter

  • Membership. one row of the lapsed and new-members export, which lists every membership the studio has ever created for this location, whatever its current state.
  • Paid. a membership whose amount paid is above zero and whose name is not a staff, complimentary, credit or referral product.
  • Lapsed. the Derived Status column as the system computes it, cross-checked against the churn and end dates on the row.
  • Lifetime. end date minus purchase date, or, for memberships still open, today minus purchase date. Open memberships are therefore treated as censored, not as survivors.
  • Usage. the Sessions Used % column, which the studio records against the classes granted, not the money paid.

The ledger starts wherever the export starts. Memberships that were created and deleted before the file’s first day are not in it, so very early churn may be understated.

No cancellation reason is recorded anywhere in these files. The reports say what happened to a membership, never why the member says it happened.

Money shown is what the studio recorded as paid for the membership, not revenue recognised; unused classes are still a liability rather than income.

A member may hold several memberships at once. Counts of memberships and counts of people are deliberately kept apart in every table.

Recommendations

14 actions, ordered by the size of the value each releases
01

Write the unspent classes back as visits, with a date on them

Front Desk + Finance

Reasoning145 memberships used a quarter or less of what they bought, leaving 1,953 classes undelivered worth about ₹3,65,59,379.

Expected impactConverting credit into booked attendance moves money the studio already holds onto the balance sheet and pulls the member back into a routine - the single cheapest retention action available.

02

Segment win-back by recency, not by lifetime value

CRM + Marketing

Reasoning219 people were here in the last thirty days and still hold ₹72,29,616; the largest group, 1,383 people, has not been inside a year.

Expected impactContact cost per recovered member falls by an order of magnitude between those two groups, and the studio currently sends the same message to both.

03

Freeze, do not lose: treat a pause request as a save

Front Desk

Reasoning821 memberships were frozen at some point and 13.2% are still live, against 5.7% of those that were never frozen.

Expected impactA member who asks to pause is planning to return. Offering the pause before they offer the cancellation converts a churn event into a deferred one.

04

Put every member on a plan at the halfway point of their membership

Studio Manager

Reasoning63% of measurable memberships attended less in their second half than their first, and 37% attended at least half as often. The change is visible before the status moves.

Expected impactA mid-membership review - attendance so far, what is left, one specific class to book - is the only intervention in this chapter that can act before the lapse rather than after it.

05

Run an activation sequence through the first fortnight

Front Desk + CRM

Reasoning44% of paid memberships end within fourteen days and 58% within thirty.

Expected impactThe first two weeks decide the membership. A dated plan, a booked class and a call on day ten is the whole intervention.

06

Collect the unused classes

CRM + Front Desk

Reasoning145 memberships used a quarter or less of what they bought, leaving 1,953 classes unspent.

Expected impactPaid-for and undelivered is a liability and a warm reason to make contact.

07

Lead with the product that keeps its members

Sales + Marketing

ReasoningSingle Class is still live or renewed 5% of the time, the best rate in the range.

Expected impactRenewal is a property of the product list before it is a property of the customer. What works should be sold first.

08

Put the churn number in the acquisition plan

Management + Finance

ReasoningMedian membership life is 18 days.

Expected impactIf most of the book has to be replaced each month, acquisition is a maintenance cost rather than a growth line, and it should be budgeted as one.

09

Make the mid-length products the default recommendation

Sales + Marketing

ReasoningMemberships of 31 to 60 days carry ₹15,306 of average cash against ₹1,907 for a week-long product.

Expected impactThe short products are the ones that leave. What a member buys first predicts how long they stay far better than how much they liked the trial.

10

Convert the single-class buyers before the week is out

Front Desk + CRM

Reasoning1,144 memberships lasted a week or less and used 0.50 classes each.

Expected impactThis is the largest, cheapest and most time-sensitive group in the ledger. A call on day three is worth more than any win-back offer in month three.

11

Work the not-activated and grace-period files monthly

CRM + Front Desk

Reasoning352 memberships never activated and 371 sit inside the grace window.

Expected impactBoth are dated, small and contactable. A monthly standing list keeps this number from building up into a churn statistic.

12

Offer the unspent classes back as visits, not as credit

CRM

Reasoning145 memberships used a quarter or less of what they paid for, leaving 1,953 classes unspent.

Expected impactUndelivered classes are already paid for. Converting them into visits rebuilds the habit; converting them into credit only postpones the decision to leave.

13

Price a three-month product deliberately

Sales + Management

ReasoningThe median membership life is 18 days.

Expected impactIf the median life is inside the second month, a three-month product sets a different expectation and gives the studio two renewal conversations instead of one.

14

Read churn by joining month, not only by product

CRM

Reasoning8,707 memberships sit in the ledger, 3,974 of them paid.

Expected impactMembers who joined in the same month behave similarly. Cohort lines show whether retention is improving or whether the mix is merely changing.
