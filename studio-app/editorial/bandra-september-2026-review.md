# Complete Content Improvement Review

Supreme HQ, Bandra | September 2026 Performance Report

The report already contains substantial analytical depth. Its biggest opportunity is to turn that analysis into a sharper, more commercially useful management document—one that explains what happened, why it matters, what the evidence actually proves, and precisely what the team should do next.

I would retain the report's analytical depth, underlying tables and detailed evidence. However, I would substantially rewrite its narrative.

The most important change is to establish one consistent editorial direction:

More members are entering the studio, but fewer visits are being generated per member. September's commercial opportunity lies in converting that larger audience into more consistent attendance, stronger purchasing behaviour and improved use of existing capacity.

This should become the central argument connecting the nine chapters, rather than allowing each chapter to tell a slightly different story.

Below are my recommended changes, including replacement text you can use directly.

## Chapter 04 — Memberships, Lapsed Members and Churn

This chapter contains some of the most useful analysis in the report, but it also has the greatest risk of misinterpretation.

A historical membership ending does not necessarily mean a customer churned. A member may have purchased another product, changed studios, exhausted their package or simply completed a short-duration membership.

I would completely change its framing.

### Recommended chapter headline

Membership expiry is visible. Disengagement needs to be identified earlier.

## Membership Health, Retention and Lapse

### Understanding the difference between product expiry, declining engagement and genuine customer loss.

The studio's historical membership ledger contains 8,707 records covering 3,768 individuals, including 3,974 paid membership records with approximately ₹4.41 crore in recorded purchase value.

The median recorded duration of paid memberships is 18 days. However, duration alone should not be interpreted as customer retention: short-duration products naturally reach their end dates faster than longer memberships.

A more useful indicator emerges from actual usage.

Among 802 paid memberships with available usage data, 145 had utilised no more than 25% of their entitlement, leaving 1,953 recorded classes unused.

This represents a meaningful customer-engagement opportunity, subject to validating whether the remaining credits are still redeemable.

The analysis should distinguish three commercially different customer groups:

1. Members whose products are approaching expiry but whose attendance remains healthy.
2. Members with valid entitlements whose attendance or usage is declining.
3. Members whose products have ended without a subsequent recorded purchase.

These groups require different interventions. Managing them through one blanket lapsed-member campaign risks approaching customers too late or with the wrong message.

**Retention priority:** Identify declining engagement while the customer still has an active reason to return, rather than treating expiry as the first indication that attention is required.

### Replace "The two ways to lose a member"

New heading: Product Completion Is Not the Same as Customer Churn

A membership can end for several reasons, and those outcomes have different commercial implications.

A customer who fully utilises a package and subsequently purchases another product demonstrates a different behaviour from one who makes no use of their entitlement and does not return.

Similarly, an expired one-month membership should not automatically be treated as failed retention unless subsequent purchasing and attendance have been examined.

The most useful classification combines product status, usage, latest visit, subsequent purchases and the time elapsed since expiry.

This allows the studio to distinguish successful product completion, potential renewal opportunities, early disengagement and genuine customer inactivity.

### Replace "The decline that comes first"

New heading: Declining Visit Frequency Is an Earlier Retention Signal

Among 1,734 memberships with sufficient attendance history for comparison, 63% recorded fewer visits in the second half of their usage period than in the first. For 37%, attendance declined by at least half.

This pattern suggests that reduced engagement may become visible before a membership reaches its end date.

Rather than waiting for a product to expire, the studio should identify customers whose recent attendance has fallen materially below their own established visiting pattern.

A frequency-based alert can initiate an earlier, more relevant conversation: understanding changes in availability, addressing scheduling difficulties and helping members plan their remaining sessions.

The objective is to prevent disengagement while meaningful options to improve the customer experience are still available.

### Change the treatment of unused credits

Do not use the heading "₹3.74 crore of value sitting in members' hands."

Replace it with:

1,953 Unused Classes: An Opportunity to Rebuild Engagement

Unused credits represent outstanding service entitlements, not automatically incremental revenue. Their commercial significance is the opportunity to reconnect customers with a product they have already purchased.

The team should prioritise members with valid remaining credits, low utilisation and a widening gap since their last attendance.

### D. Fix the chapter-level metric definitions

I would add a small, consistent methodology note to every chapter, using the following template:

### Reading the Numbers

**Reporting period:** September 1–30, 2026, unless otherwise indicated.

**Comparison basis:** September 2025 for year-on-year movements.

**Sales revenue:** Recorded transactions attributed to the payment date.

**Delivered class revenue:** Revenue attributed to attended sessions in the class export. This is a separate measure from cash sales.

## 11. My recommended implementation order

If you're updating the report-generation system, I would make the changes in this sequence:

First: Fix calculation and interpretation errors

Resolve conflicting totals, historical-versus-monthly comparisons, incorrect percentage-point descriptions and unsupported financial opportunity estimates.

Second: Rewrite every chapter introduction

Use the nine replacement introductions above. Each should contain a performance verdict, supporting figures, commercial interpretation and a clear management implication.

Third: Consolidate recommendations

Remove repetitive chapter-ending actions and introduce the seven-priority October Action Plan. Retain chapter-specific recommendations only when they add a genuinely distinct intervention.

Fourth: Introduce better performance measurement

Add the October Scorecard, distinguish targets from forecasts, and give each major recommendation a defined success indicator.

Finally: Apply the editorial improvements throughout

Standardise terminology, remove broken generated sentences, improve chart captions and give the report a consistent, polished management voice.

The single most valuable structural change: Make the Executive Summary and Studio Management Scorecard the decision-making layer, with the remaining chapters serving as the supporting investigation.

That would transform this from a lengthy performance analysis into a much more useful monthly business review—one leadership can read quickly, studio managers can act upon, and the team can return to the following month to measure what actually improved.
