Start here

The till held. What sat behind it did not.

September 2026 banked ₹15,29,410 across 213 transactions — a fall of just 2.3% on September 2025 and, read on its own, an unremarkable month. The average sale actually rose, from ₹7,178 to ₹7,180, and the median sale rose from ₹1,770 to ₹1,942. On the headline numbers this is a steady month.

Three things underneath it are not steady, and they are what this report is about.

The one-line version, if you read nothing else. The studio served 394 members this September against 305 last September — its widest September base in three years — and collected ₹3,316 of class revenue from each of them against ₹3,218. Both of those went up. The only thing that went down is how often each of them came: 3.56 visits against 4.84. That single change is worth more than the entire year-on-year movement in the cash line, and it is the difference between a studio running at capacity and one running at 42.6%.

First, the month was funded by people the studio already had. Members making their first-ever Bandra purchase contributed ₹3,00,229, or 19.6% of the month; existing members contributed the other ₹12,29,182. That is normal for a mature studio, and it is genuinely good news about the base. It also means September’s cash figure tells you almost nothing about September’s acquisition performance. The two must be read separately.

A note on that number: 19.6% counts members whose first purchase at Bandra was in September. The performance review quotes 12.5% on a different and narrower definition — clients whose first visit was in September, who paid a narrower sum. The two measures diverge because some September visitors had already bought a package, and some September buyers had already visited. Both are correct; the purchase-based figure is the relevant one for a sales report.

Second, the commitment length is shortening. Three-month memberships sold 18 units in April 2025 and 2 in September 2026. One-month memberships fell from 29 units to 15 over the same year. The products that give the studio a forward revenue cushion are being sold less often than they were.

Third — and this is the finding with the most consequence — the studio is now selling almost exactly what it delivers. In September 2024, ₹23,96,991 of cash translated into ₹7,73,072 of attended classes: 32%. In September 2025 that rose to 63%. In September 2026 it reached 85%. That is a profound structural change and it deserves to be named plainly, because it cuts both ways.

The same flat top line means something completely different in 2026 than it did in 2024

Two years ago a large share of the cash the studio took was never drawn down as classes. Members bought packages and long memberships, attended less than they paid for, and the studio kept the difference. That unused capacity was quiet, real revenue — and it was also a symptom of disengagement.

By September 2026 that cushion has largely gone: 85 of every ₹100 banked is now delivered as an attended class, against 32 two years earlier. The studio is selling shorter, cheaper, more-consumable products to members who use them. The revenue is more honest. It is also more fragile, because the same flat cash figure now has to be earned every month rather than partly collected in advance.

This is the single most useful reframe in the report. Bandra’s September problem is not that revenue collapsed — it did not. It is that the business model quietly shifted from selling commitment to selling consumption, while the cash total stayed flat and therefore hid the change. Everything the studio needs to fix about retention now has a shorter runway than it did, because there is far less prepaid, unused membership to absorb a bad month.

The base is widening

More people came to Bandra this September than in either of the last two. They each came less often.

This is the finding that reframes the whole month. The cash line fell 2.3%, and it is tempting to read that as demand weakening. The check-in ledger says the opposite: 394 individual members trained at Bandra in September 2026, against 305 in September 2025 and 355 in September 2024 — a base that has grown 29% year on year. The studio is reaching more people than it ever has in a September.

What fell was frequency. The average member trained 3.56 times in September 2026 against 4.84 times a year earlier. Revenue per active member went the other way — ₹3,316 against ₹3,218, up 3.0%. Each member is worth more; there are just fewer visits in each of them. Fewer seats were sold, but they were better-paid seats.

The loyal core halved its frequency

Seventy-eight people who trained here last September came back this September. Between them they gave the studio 252 fewer visits.

This is treated in full in the class performance report, because it is fundamentally a question of volume rather than of money. Its cash consequence is easy to state, though. The 252 visits those members did not make are, at September’s class revenue of ₹931 a head, roughly ₹2,34,491 of class revenue the studio never booked — in a month when the entire cash line moved by ₹35,322. Frequency, not price, is where the money went.

A membership business has two jobs: get paid, and get the member to re-commit. The second job is the one that compounds. Bandra’s membership line is being held up by one-month products while the longer commitments — the ones that roll over less often and generate far more lifetime value per conversation — shrink to almost nothing.

Three-month memberships are the clearest case: 18 units in April 2025, 2 units in September 2026. In the same window one-month units fell from 29 to 15. Only the new six-month and annual tiers grew, and from a base of almost zero. This is a sales-mix problem, not a demand problem — the members are still buying; they are buying shorter.

Why this matters more than the flat top line suggests

A one-month member makes twelve purchase decisions a year. A three-month member makes four. A twelve-month member makes one. Every step down in commitment length multiplies the number of moments at which the studio can lose the member — and the data shows September losing them in exactly those moments. Combined with a delivery ratio that has risen from 32% to 85%, the studio now has both a shorter commitment base and less prepaid cushion to absorb the consequences.

How the money arrived

Online payments now carry the largest tickets by a factor of two, and discounting is almost entirely code-driven.

Four channels took the month’s money. Counter and custom payment took the most transactions — 151 sales worth ₹8,92,920 — but the largest average ticket sits with Stripe at ₹12,820 on 28 sales, more than twice the cash average of ₹4,780. The pattern is not incidental: the products that require a decision — annual and six-month memberships, multi-class packs — are bought on cards and online, while day-to-day singles are bought at the desk. Stripe transactions fell from 53 to 28 year on year; multi-method sales, where a member splits a large purchase, doubled from 6 to 13.

Discounting is concentrated in five codes, and three of them are acquisition codes rather than retention codes. Two codes alone — NC-2026-SHQ (20 uses, ₹61,129 given away) and ZAP10 (14 uses, ₹53,380) — account for ₹1,14,509 of the ₹1,63,709 given away. A single use of the 20OFFSHQ code cost ₹41,800 on its own. Because a code requires intent, every rupee of discount here is a rupee someone asked for and was granted — which makes it a policy question, not a leakage question.

Who the money came from

Ten members account for more than a third of the month. Half of all buyers account for seven per cent.

September’s 139 paying members are not a flat distribution. The top ten by spend account for 38% of the month’s cash, the top twenty for 52%, while the bottom half of buyers contribute 7.3%. That is not unusual for a boutique studio and it is not a problem — but it does mean that two or three decisions by high-value members move the monthly number more than any campaign aimed at the base. The single largest purchase of the month, an annual unlimited membership at ₹1,75,560, was worth more than the combined purchases of the bottom 48 buyers.

The other side of concentration is breadth of need. 83% of September buyers bought from only one product category. Only 23 of 139 members bought across two categories, and a single member bought across four. There is very little cross-selling happening: a member who joins for Barre and a member who joins for PowerCycle are, on this month’s evidence, treated as the same customer until they leave.

What the first purchase predicts

Nothing in the sales ledger separates members as sharply as the product they started on — except how quickly they came back.

Take the 4,218 people whose first visit to Bandra is on record and group them by the first thing they bought. The spread in eventual lifetime value is not marginal, it is structural. A member who started on a 1 Month Unlimited is worth ₹74,283 to the studio. One who started on a Single Class is worth ₹13,689. Between them sit every other product in the range, in a consistent order: memberships first, packages second, single classes last.

The obvious caution applies, and it is worth stating plainly: members who chose a bigger commitment were already more committed. But the gap is 5.4-fold, and the studio sells both products from the same front desk within the same minute. What a new client is offered first is not a neutral choice.

The second predictor is time, and it is even more directly actionable. Of every first-timer on record, the speed at which they took a second class divides their eventual value almost as sharply as their first purchase did. Members who returned within a week are worth ₹27,745 on average; those who took a month, or never came back at all, are worth ₹13,918.

Recommendations

Thirteen actions, ordered by expected value

The first three protect what already works. Four are pricing and structure. The last four attack frequency, channel and cross-selling — the levers that do not require a single new customer. Each recommendation carries the reasoning behind it and the impact the studio should expect, so it can be argued with rather than simply accepted.

01

Re-weight the product ladder toward 12-class packages

Sales + Studio Manager

ReasoningThe 12-class package sells at ₹1,444 a class — the best value the studio offers — and it grew +₹68,740 on four more sales. The 10-class package costs the member ₹1,735 a class and sold 13 units at almost exactly the same average ticket. Both price at roughly ₹17,300. A member choosing the 10-class package over the 12 is paying the same money for two fewer classes.

Expected impactMoving 6 of September’s 13 ten-class sales up to the twelve-class tier adds 12 class credits to the studio’s delivered base at no revenue cost, and is the cleanest available improvement to the entry offer. On September volume that is worth about ₹1.1 lakh of delivered capacity a year.

02

Put a named owner on every repeat sale

Studio Manager

Reasoning41 of September’s 139 paying members bought more than once — and they produced 34.1% of the month’s ₹15,29,410. Meanwhile ₹3,66,511 of September sales — 24% — have no salesperson recorded, the largest single block in the ledger.

Expected impactNamed attribution on the unattributed block would make the repeat-purchase cycle visible and manageable for the first time. Selling one additional package to each of the 98 single-purchase members at September’s median ticket of ₹1,942 is worth roughly ₹1.9 lakh a month.

03

Fix the three-month membership before it disappears

Sales + Finance

ReasoningThree-month memberships ran at 18 units in April 2025 and 2 units in September 2026. Over the same period one-month units fell from 29 to 15. Long-duration commitments — the product that produces the studio’s cash cushion — are being sold less often in every length except the new annual tier.

Expected impactRecovering even 8 three-month units a month at the historic average ticket of about ₹24,900 returns roughly ₹24 lakh a year of higher-commitment revenue, and materially improves the deferred-revenue position.

04

Standardise discount depth at 15% and stop it leaking past it

Finance + Front Desk

Reasoning36 September transactions carried a discount worth ₹1,63,709 in total — 9.7% of list value. Depth averages 15.0%, but the annual membership was discounted 19.2% and the 20-class pack 19.2%, against 14.4% on the standard packages.

Expected impactHolding every discount to the 15% standard while keeping the same volume saves roughly ₹1.1 lakh a year on September’s pattern alone. Depth discipline is also the single easiest lever to audit.

05

Treat the single-class price as a deliberate tier, not a default

Sales

ReasoningSingle classes at ₹1,943 each are now the second-largest unit line at 38 sales, up +₹20,799 year on year. They are profitable per head but they never build a habit, and ₹1,943 a class is the worst value the member can pick.

Expected impactConverting a third of September’s 38 single-class buyers into a four-class package at the point of purchase adds about ₹56,000 of committed revenue from the same people, and gives them a reason to return.

06

Method and limitations

How to read these numbers, and what they cannot show

Sources and method

Three exports at a snapshot of 11 October 2026. Sales – Raw (25,044 succeeded payments network-wide, 6,547 at Bandra) provides cash received, product, discount, salesperson and payment method. Day End Report – Part 3 – Sessions (25,675 class occurrences) provides revenue attributed to attended classes. Day End Report – Part 1 – New (21,171 member records) identifies which September revenue came from clients acquired that month.

What the two revenue measures mean, and why both are shown

Cash is money actually received on a payment date; it is what the studio banked. Class-attributed revenue is what the platform allocates to each attended class; it is what the studio delivered. They are different numbers on purpose, and the gap between them is the most informative thing in this report — it measures how much of what members paid for has not yet been consumed. A widening gap means members are holding unused credits; a narrowing gap means the studio is selling closer to what it delivers.

Known limitations

  • No cost of sale. No CAC, contribution margin or payroll cost is present in this data, so payback and profitability cannot be calculated. All figures are gross value.
  • Salesperson cover. 24% of September revenue has no salesperson recorded. Attributed performance is therefore a floor on each named seller, not a full picture.
  • Retail is understated in scope. Retail appears here as a revenue line only; no stock, cost or sell-through data is available.
  • Comparability. September 2025 followed Ganesh Chaturthi (27 August); September 2026 absorbed it (14 September). Single-month year-on-year comparisons carry that distortion. The year-to-date table is the more reliable frame.
