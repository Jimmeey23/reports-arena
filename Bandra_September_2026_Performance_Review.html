Start here

September is an outlier inside a steady year — not the start of a decline

The most important fact about September 2026 is what it is not. It is not the month the studio's fundamentals broke. Over the twelve months to September, Bandra's second-visit rate held at 29.2% versus 29.1% the year before, and the revenue generated per new client within 90 days rose to ₹2,431 from ₹2,259 — the second consecutive annual improvement. Volume across the trailing twelve months was effectively flat, at 1,439 clients against 1,475.

Year-to-date the picture is equally reassuring on the metric that matters most. Across January to September 2026, the share of new clients who returned for a second class was 39.1%, against 39.2% in 2025 and 37.4% in 2024. Whatever went wrong in September, it did not go wrong for the year.

One qualification belongs here rather than in a footnote, because it cuts across every retention figure in this report and is quantified in full later. Roughly a third of what the studio records as a "second visit" is the pre-paid second half of a Newcomers 2 For 1 intro pack — a class the client has already bought and is simply using up. These year-level rates are therefore the right measure for spotting change over time, because the same distortion runs through all three years, but they overstate the absolute level of genuine repeat demand. Where a true return rate is quoted below, the pre-paid visits have been removed.

What September did was concentrate a set of already-visible weaknesses into a single month, in a way that is now measurable and therefore fixable. This edition adds three further sources — the full 150,895-row check-in ledger, the 25,315-record lead register and the 25,044-record sales ledger — which together make it possible to say not just what happened in September but where it happened and who it happened to.

The short version of what those sources add. September's revenue was carried almost entirely by members the studio already had, which is why the month looked normal in the accounts while its future quietly thinned. Nearly six in ten of the month's failed leads were simply never reached, or asked for more time. The studio's second-visit rate is overstated by the intro pack, which pre-pays the second visit. And the single most consequential change at Bandra this year was not in marketing or pricing at all: it was who was standing at the front of the room.

The number that explains the others

The month looked fine in the accounts because the existing base paid for it

Two true statements that look like they contradict each other

The sales ledger shows September 2026 taking ₹15,29,410 across 196 transactions — down only 2.3% on September 2025 and, in isolation, an unremarkable month. The member cohort file shows the 143 clients acquired in September generating ₹2,35,543 of cash so far, against ₹9,44,839 for the 98 clients acquired a year earlier.

Both are correct, and the distance between them is the single most useful thing in this review. 87.5% of September's revenue came from members the studio already had, and only 12.5% from anyone new. September's till looked healthy because the existing base kept paying. September's future got visibly thinner.

This is why the two figures should be read together rather than instead of one another. A flat top line is genuinely good news — it means the studio is not losing its existing members, which is the harder and more valuable half of the business. But it also means the ledger was masking the acquisition problem completely. Had this month's new-client shortfall been read only through the till, it would have been invisible.

The September picture

Acquisition succeeded. Follow-through did not.

Volume was the month's clear success. At 143 new clients, September 2026 was the studio's strongest September in the dataset — 44% above 2025 and 22% above the trailing monthly average — and Bandra's share of group-wide intake climbed to 36.6% from 27.7% a year earlier. The studio is doing more than its share of the network's front-end work.

What did not follow was return visits. Restricting comparison to clients who visited in the first 13 days of the month — so that every one of them has had at least 28 days to come back — 12.9% of September clients returned for a second class within 30 days, against a 29.4% norm. That single number accounts for almost all of the month's value loss, because the second visit is by far the strongest predictor of everything downstream.

What went well

Four things September got right — and should keep

Conversion speed was the fastest on record

Clients who converted in September did so in a median of 3 days, against an all-time Bandra median of 8. Two-thirds converted within a week. When intent was present, the studio captured it quickly and without friction — a genuine operational strength worth protecting.

Those who did convert bought larger packages

The median first purchase among September converters was ₹16,734 — the highest of any month in the past year, against ₹14,222 in August and ₹9,639 in July. Half bought the Studio 12-Class Package, compared with 23% historically. September's converters were fewer but higher-intent and higher-value.

Paid-entry clients performed exactly to norm

Clients who paid for their first visit converted at 25.0%, against a 25.8% historical rate, at ₹9,824 of value each. This is the studio's smallest entry channel at just 4 clients — and its most reliable. It is a case for considered expansion, not retreat.

Barre 57 held the line, and several trainers excelled

Barre 57 delivered 59% of the month's revenue and the studio's best format-level conversion at 13.3%. Individually, Anmol Sharma placed 6 of 14 new clients into a second class — a 42.9% follow-through rate that is the strongest individual result in the studio this month, and proof the model works when it is executed well.

Root cause

Five explanations tested against the data

Eight candidate explanations were examined — the ones that are obvious, and the ones that are easy to miss. Four do not survive contact with the numbers. Three do, and they turn out to be linked.

Confirmed — first order

The teaching roster turned over

Five trainers who carried a large share of client-facing classes in September 2025 — Cauveri Vikrant, Karanvir Bhatia, Karan Bhatia, Simonelle De Vitre and Mrigakshi Jaiswal — took 65% fewer classes in September 2026 (1,112 down to 394). The group that absorbed that load rose from 221 classes to 545, up 147%. Their clients are worth materially different amounts: ₹8,674 per new client for the departing group against ₹6,694 for the incoming one.

The largest single change at Bandra between the two Septembers was not demand, price or marketing. It was an unmeasured swap of the people who deliver the first class — the one moment that determines whether a client ever comes back.
Confirmed

One trainer held 23% of the month's intake, and none returned

Reshma Sharma received 33 of September's 143 new clients — 23% of the entire month. Not one attended a second class, anywhere in the network. Trial-for-trial, her clients converted at 0 of 18 against 9 of 61 (14.8%) for the rest of the studio.

Her all-time record is unremarkable rather than poor, which is the point: a zero month can happen to anyone, and the studio should never have a quarter of its acquisition resting on one person with no second look.
Confirmed

The lead pipeline stopped closing

Leads rose 40% year-on-year while wins fell 48%. Win rate collapsed from 17.0% to 6.3%. Of the 2,572 leads that closed as lost with a reason recorded, 38.4% were simply unreachable and 20.1% were postponements; only 2.1% cited price.

Interest is not the constraint. Follow-up is. This is the cheapest problem in the review to fix and the one with the shortest payback.
Ruled out

Ganesh Chaturthi disrupted the month

Ganesh Chaturthi fell on 14 September, with visarjan on the 25th — and Bandra sits on the procession route. But splitting September into festival phases, the pre-festival window converted at 7.1%, no better than the festival window at 6.2%. A festival suppresses a studio for ten days; it does not suppress the week before it. The weakness was already present on 1 September.

September 2024 shows the same test with the opposite result: pre-festival 46.7%, festival 24.5%. The pattern is not stable enough to be causal.
Ruled out

Demand fell across the market

On the identical matched basis, Kwality House converted at 13.8% and Kenkere House at 30.8% while Bandra sat at 12.5%. Kenkere's conversion actually improved year-on-year. Bandra's intake also rose 44%, which is not the signature of a soft market.

The dip is studio-specific, not market-wide.
Ruled out

Volume overloaded the team

Across 21 months at Bandra, monthly volume correlates with conversion rate at just +0.06 and with second-visit rate at -0.11. Some of the studio's strongest months were also among its highest-volume.

High intake is not, by itself, a cause of poor conversion. This matters: it points away from staffing and effort.
Confirmed

Intake mix shifted toward low-intent entry

Hosted-class share against second-visit rate correlates at -0.62 across 21 months — the strongest relationship in the data. Free-entry share at -0.58, weekend share at -0.41. September combined a 23.8% hosted share with a 52.4% weekend share, and 18.2% free entry.

This is a mix problem, not a demand or effort problem — and mix is directly controllable.
Ruled out

Capacity or attendance was down

Bandra ran 251 classes in September 2026 against 266 a year earlier, filled 1,404 seats against 1,476, at 44.0% capacity utilisation against 43.0%. Average class size was 5.6 against 5.5.

The studio ran very close to a normal schedule with a normal audience. Footfall fell only 4.9% — there was no shortage of people in the room.
Contributing

Follow-up execution on the 12–13 September weekend

The month's single largest intake block — 30 clients in two days, 21% of the month, against a daily average of 4.8 — produced zero conversions and ₹709 of revenue. Thirteen were attendees of one partner event, none of whom returned.

It accounts for 82% of the month's conversion gap on 43% of the cohort. A specific, datable, avoidable trigger.

The finding that was hiding

Who was teaching, and what happened to the clients they received

The most consequential change at Bandra between September 2025 and September 2026 was not in marketing, pricing or demand. It was who was standing at the front of the room.

Five trainers who carried a large share of the studio's client-facing load in 2025 — Cauveri Vikrant, Karanvir Bhatia, Karan Bhatia, Simonelle De Vitre and Mrigakshi Jaiswal — between them took 1,112 classes in September 2025 and 394 in September 2026, a fall of 65%. The newer group that absorbed that load — Reshma Sharma, Pranjali Jain, Anmol Sharma and Rohan Dahima — rose from 221 classes to 545, an increase of 147%. Total studio bookings fell 24.8% over the same period.

This matters because the trainers who left or reduced their hours were not interchangeable with those who replaced them on the measure that drives the business. Across their full history, the five departing trainers brought in new clients who were worth ₹8,674 on average. The four who replaced them have brought in clients worth ₹6,694 — a difference of roughly ₹1,981 per new client.

It is worth being precise about what is and is not being claimed. This is not an argument that the incoming trainers are worse teachers. It is the narrower, harder observation that a studio's first-class experience is delivered by a person, that not all first classes are equally effective at producing a second one, and that Bandra conducted a large, unmeasured swap of exactly that variable in the twelve months to September.

One trainer received 23.1% of September's new clients. None of them came back.

Reshma Sharma took 33 of the month's 143 new clients — 23.1% of the entire month's intake, more than double any other trainer. Every one of those 33 clients attended exactly one class. Across the entire group network, not a single one of them ever checked in again — not at Bandra, not anywhere. The other 110 clients taught by the rest of the studio returned at 21.8%, averaging 1.76 visits against her group's 1.00.

This deserves to be stated carefully and in full, because it is the kind of finding that is easy to over-read.

Her all-time record at Bandra is not exceptional either way: 360 clients, a 21.1% conversion rate against a studio average of 23.8%, and she has had genuinely strong months — 77.8% in July 2024, 53.8% in December 2024. On lifetime value per client she ranks 2 of 10 among trainers with 150 or more clients, at ₹6,942. A zero month can happen to anyone.

What cannot be waved away is the arithmetic of the concentration. To test whether the result was genuinely meaningful or simply an unlucky sequence, her clients can be compared to the rest of the studio on the narrowest possible basis — trial-class clients only, the same entry type, the same month:

  • Trial clients taught by Reshma Sharma: 18 clients, 0 converted.
  • Trial clients taught by everyone else: 61 clients, 9 converted — 14.8%.

At the rest of the studio's rate, 33 clients would have been expected to yield 3.6 new members. If that probability is taken as the baseline, the chance of drawing zero conversions from 33 consecutive clients purely by luck is about 2.2% — roughly one in fifty. On the sharper return-visit measure the arithmetic is starker still: had her 33 clients returned at the 21.8% rate the rest of the studio achieved, that would be seven people; the probability of seeing none by chance is well under one in a thousand.

The genuinely useful point is not about one trainer's month. It is that a single instructor held 23.1% of the studio's new-client intake with no hedge. Whatever the cause of a zero month — personal circumstances, an off-period, a mismatch between her coaching style and the trial population, a scheduling accident — the studio had no second look at those clients. A quarter of the month's acquisition pipeline passed through one pair of hands and was never reviewed by anyone else.

That concentration is itself the finding, and it is directly fixable: cap any one trainer's share of first visits, and route every trial client to a second touchpoint — a different class format, a different trainer, a follow-up call — before the relationship is allowed to end.

The pipeline problem

More interest than ever, and less of it being closed

If September's conversion problem were a demand problem, the lead log would show fewer people walking in. It shows the opposite. Bandra generated 189 leads in September 2026 against 135 in September 2025 — up 40% — while the number of those leads that became paying members fell from 23 to 12, down 48%. Win rate fell from 17.0% to 6.3%. The pipeline is fuller than a year ago and leaking far worse.

This reframes the diagnosis considerably. There is no shortage of interest in the studio. Something is happening between a lead arriving and a membership being sold — and the timing of that break is visible in the monthly series, which begins diverging in early 2026 and has not recovered.

Two details keep this from being as bleak as it reads. First, 120 of the 189 September leads — 63.5% — were still open, uncategorised, or had trialled without yet buying at the time of this data. September's pipeline is not closed; it is unfinished, and much of it is still winnable. Second, the reasons leads go cold are almost entirely recoverable:

The hosted-class programme is where this is clearest. 1,179 Bandra hosted leads have completed trials at a rate of 75.8% — the highest trial rate of any source in the studio. Only 8.3% of them then bought. Client referrals, by contrast, trial at essentially the same rate (78.4%) and convert at 24.2% — 2.9 times better — and are worth ₹8,762 per lead against ₹3,445.

So the same number of trials produces three times the sales when the person arrived because someone they trusted told them to. That is not a property of the trial. The intro pack, and an honest second-visit rate

The cheapest class the studio sells, and what it actually buys

The second-visit rate is overstated, and the intro pack is why

Of the 24 September clients who attended a second class, 11 were simply redeeming the second half of a pre-paid Studio Newcomers 2 For 1 pack they had already bought. That visit cost the studio a class and required no decision from the client. Only 13 of the 24 were genuine, unprompted returns.

This is the user-facing arithmetic: the headline September second-visit rate of 16.8% falls to 9.1% once pre-paid redemptions are removed. Put differently: 46% of what looked like returning demand was a class someone had already paid for. Across the trailing eleven months the same effect runs through roughly 41% of all recorded second visits.

The reason this is not a technicality is that the 2-for-1 is not a neutral product. It is priced at ₹1,855 for two classes — ₹927 a class, against ₹1,738 for a single class and ₹1,278 per class inside the 12-class package. It is the cheapest class the studio sells, by a wide margin. That is a defensible acquisition price. The question is what it acquires.

The numbers are uncomfortable. Of 807 Bandra members who have bought the intro pack, only 37.5% have ever bought anything else. The median 2-for-1 entrant's total lifetime spend at Bandra is ₹1,838 — which is to say, the intro pack and almost nothing after it. Members who entered through any other product have a median lifetime value of ₹10,628 and a mean of ₹29,159 against ₹15,787. Only 52 of 807 ever bought a membership.

The honest interpretation is that the 2-for-1 is doing one job well and another badly. As a low-friction trial it works — it gets people through the door at a price they will pay, which is exactly what it is for. But it is not producing members, and it is distorting the studio's own retention metrics while it does so. It flatters the second-visit rate in every month it operates, which means the studio’s true rate of genuine repeat demand is lower than any of its dashboards show. A 2-for-1 buyer who returns for class two has not yet made a single repeat-purchase decision.

The correction matters most when the same adjustment is applied to both sides of the comparison, which is the only way to read it fairly. On the canonical matched window — first visits in the opening thirteen days of the month — the ledger says 12.9% of September clients returned against 34.9% on the trailing eleven-month norm, a gap of 22.1 points. Strip out the pre-paid redemptions from both sides and the figures become 8.6% against 20.7% — a gap of 12.2 points.

Both readings matter, and they point in different directions. The absolute level is worse than it looks: September's genuine second-visit rate of 8.6% is just over a third of the 20.7% the studio actually achieves once the coupon effect is removed from its own history. But the fall is less severe than the headline suggests, because the pre-paid distortion flatters the baseline more than it flatters September (40.7% of nested returns are pre-paid historically against 33.3% in September). The honest summary is that the studio's normal retention is genuinely strong — 20.7% real returns is a good number for any boutique studio — and September delivered less than half of it.

The practical consequence is a definitional one that the studio should adopt immediately: a client who bought a two-class intro pack becomes a genuine returning client on their third visit, not their second. On that definition Bandra's numbers look different, and more honest, across every month in the dataset.

What the studio did not see

Three second-order effects, and one structural risk

Some obvious explanations are worth stating plainly, even where they turn out to be wrong, because ruling them out is what makes the remaining explanation credible — and because two of them turn out to be right.

The studio ran a normal schedule at normal class sizes

Bandra held 273 classes in September 2025 and 259 in September 2026. Seats offered fell 6.7%, from 3,530 to 3,293. Capacity utilisation was 41.8% last year and 42.6% this year; average class size was 5.4 then and 5.4 now. September 2026 was not a month of empty rooms or a thinned-out timetable. It was very close to September 2025 in operational shape.

This is genuinely useful, because it removes the largest alternative explanation. The month did not fail for want of capacity or for want of attendance — 1,404 people attended classes in September 2026 against 1,476 a year earlier, a fall of only 4.9%. Footfall was essentially intact.

The same footfall is being spread across more people, and that is the hidden cost

Here is the second-order effect that is genuinely easy to miss. Attendance held roughly flat while the number of distinct members attending it rose. Bandra drew 1,404 attended visits from 394 members in September 2026, against 1,476 from 305 members in September 2025. Visits per attending member fell from 4.84 to 3.56 — and 3.38 for September on the full-month basis, the lowest monthly figure of 2026.

The studio looks equally busy at the same level of effort. Underneath, the same number of bodies is composed of more, shallower relationships. That is a quieter and more consequential shift than a visible drop in attendance, and it points at the same underlying issue as the conversion shortfall: the studio is getting people in the door and not keeping them in the room.

Late cancellations have been genuinely fixed

One notable operational improvement deserves credit, and it is easy to overlook because it is a number that got better. The no-show and late-cancellation rate fell from 30.8% in September 2025 to 12.5% in September 2026. That is a structural improvement rather than noise — the rate has stayed near 12% every month since the change — and it returns real bookable capacity to the schedule without a single extra class being added. Against a background of harder acquisition, this is a genuine operational win and it should be protected.

On the fully matured slice of the September cohort — the 70 clients who visited between 1 and 13 September, each observed for at least 28 days — the trailing norm predicts 10.5 thirty-day conversions. 5 occurred.

The distribution of that shortfall is the most important finding in this review. The first eleven days of September performed essentially to norm — 5 conversions against 5.7 expected, a gap of less than one client across 40 people. The entire remaining shortfall, 82% of it, sits in the weekend of 12–13 September, which absorbed 30 clients in two days and produced none of the month's conversions.

Scaled across all 143 September clients, the gap amounts to roughly 11 clients who would normally have become members. Measured in first-purchase revenue over the next few months that is about ₹1,16,974; measured across the lifetime of those relationships, at the studio's observed conversion economics, it is approximately ₹3,15,243. Separately, the 10 missing second visits in the matured slice represent around 6 further conversions — about ₹1,82,710 of lifetime value that is still recoverable through follow-up rather than acquisition.

That decomposition uses the ledger's own visit counts, and the intro pack touches it too. On the same matched window the genuine second-visit rate is 8.6% rather than the published 12.9%, because 3 of the nine returns were pre-paid 2-for-1 redemptions. Setting that alongside a baseline adjusted the same way — 20.7% genuine returns historically — the true gap on the matured slice is about 8.5 returns rather than 10.5, worth roughly ₹1,48,348 of recoverable lifetime value. The correction narrows the distance from normal but sharpens the level: September's genuine return rate is barely a third of the studio's own.

Seen this way, September is not a story of systemic failure, and it is certainly not a story about the studio's everyday operation. Ordinary trading in the first eleven days was sound. Two days of concentrated, complimentary, event-driven intake absorbed a fifth of the month's clients, returned none of them, and accounted for effectively the whole of the month's value loss. That is a schedulable, preventable event — which is why it is the first recommendation in this report rather than an acceptance of decline.

Recommendations

Eight actions, ordered by expected value

These are ordered so that the first three can be acted on this week without spending anything. The two that involve spending or pricing — the intro pack and the channel mix — sit in the middle deliberately: they are worth more, but they should follow the free fixes rather than lead them.

01

Cap any single trainer’s share of new-client intake, and guarantee every new client a second touchpoint

Studio Manager + Head of Training

ReasoningReshma Sharma received 23% of September’s new clients — 33 of 143 — and not one of them returned for a second class, against a 22% return rate for the rest of the studio. Trial-for-trial the comparison is 0 of 18 against 9 of 61 (14.8%). Her all-time record is ordinary rather than poor, which is precisely the point: any trainer can have a zero month, and the studio should never have 23% of a month’s intake resting on one person with no second look.

Expected impactConverting her 33 clients at the rest of the studio’s rate would have produced 3.6 more members and lifted September from 8.4% to 10.9% conversion on its own. Applied as a standing rule, it removes the single largest concentration risk in the acquisition funnel at no cost.

02

Rebuild the trial follow-up around contact, not pricing

Studio Manager + Front Desk

Reasoning38% of Bandra leads that fail to close are simply unreachable, and a further 13% are postponements — "will get back to us", "asked to call back later". Only 2% cite price. Meanwhile wins fell from 23 to 12 (-48%) on leads that rose +40%. The blocking constraint is the follow-up sequence, not the offer.

Expected impact120 of September’s 189 leads (63%) were still open at the time of this data. Recovering even a quarter of them at the studio’s historical win rate is roughly 7 additional members from a pipeline that already exists.

03

Route every new client to a second trainer and a second class format

Studio Manager

ReasoningA second visit multiplies conversion odds 57-fold and is worth ₹24,744 in lifetime value. September proved that a first class is not a reliable predictor of a second one when it is the only exposure a client has. Booking the second class before the client leaves — with a different trainer and a different format — converts an intention into a diary entry.

Expected impactOn the report’s canonical basis this is worth about ₹2,01,998 on the matured slice alone — 11.6 missing returns. Corrected on both sides using the ledger, September’s genuine return rate is 8.6% against a 20.7% norm, a gap of 8.5 returns worth roughly ₹1,48,348. The correction narrows the gap but deepens the level: either way, roughly ₹1.8–2.0 lakh is recoverable on this slice without acquiring a single new client.

04

Re-price or re-purpose the 2-for-1 intro pack

Marketing + Finance

ReasoningThe pack sells two classes for ₹1,855 — ₹927 a class, against ₹1,738 for a single class. Of 807 Bandra members who have bought one, only 37.5% have ever bought anything else, and the median entrant’s lifetime spend is ₹1,838 — effectively the pack and nothing more. Only 52 ever bought a membership.

Expected impactTwo options, both testable: attach the pack to a booked second class rather than an open two-class credit, or price it closer to a paid trial at full single-class value. Either way, stopping the pack from being counted as a repeat visit makes every retention metric in the business honest from here on.

05

Re-weight acquisition toward referral, and away from open hosted events

Marketing

ReasoningReferral leads convert at 24.2% and are worth ₹8,762 each. Hosted-class leads produce the highest trial rate in the studio at 75.8% and then convert at 8.3%, worth ₹3,445. The same volume of trials produces 2.9 times the sales when the client arrived on someone’s recommendation.

Expected impactWebsite and online sources supply 47.5% of leads at ₹4,120; calls and messages supply 11.3% at ₹10,185. Shifting a tenth of website volume into the highest-yield channels is worth several lakh of lifetime value a year.

06

Fix referral attribution before making further channel decisions

Operations / Data

Reasoning41 of 143 September clients have no source recorded and produced zero recorded value. Across all non-converting Bandra clients since January 2025, 48% of referral-class clients are unsourced against 24% of trial-class clients. The best-converting channel is also the least well measured.

Expected impactOne field, made mandatory at the point of first check-in. Until it is fixed, referral performance is guesswork and so is any budget decision that depends on it.

07

Protect the trainers the data says generate the most valuable clients

Head of Training

ReasoningThe five trainers who carried a large share of 2025 bookings took 65% fewer classes in September 2026; the newer group absorbed 147% more. Their clients are worth ₹8,674 on average against ₹6,694. Across 21 months lifetime value per client ranges from ₹3,708 to ₹25,982 — a 7.0x spread.

Expected impactLifting the five lowest-performing trainers to studio median adds ₹33,58,773 of lifetime value on existing volume with no new acquisition spend. It also argues for measuring trainers on client outcomes, not class hours.

08

Work the 170 At-Risk members this month, while the base is still carrying the studio

CRM / Retention

ReasoningThe base funded 87.5% of September’s revenue while new clients contributed 12.5%. The ten largest payers alone contributed ₹5,81,663 — 38.0% of the month. 170 members are 31–60 days inactive carrying ₹32,00,706 of prior value, and 635 are Lapsed.

Expected impactRe-engaging 20% of the At-Risk group is worth approximately ₹6.4 lakh of latent value — and it protects the base that is currently making the top line look steady.

Method and limitations

How to read these numbers, and what they cannot show

The equal-observation principle

Conversions at Bandra take time: the median happens on day 8, 49% within a week, 69% within 30 days and 81% within 90. A September cohort read in mid-October has therefore not had a fair chance to convert, and comparing its raw rate to a settled September 2025 would overstate any decline considerably. Every rate in this review is computed on an equal-observation basis — a 30-day window, or first visits restricted to days 1–13 of the month so every client has at least 28 days of history. Raw headline rates appear only where they are explicitly labelled.

Why September-to-September comparisons need care

Ganesh Chaturthi fell on 27 August in 2025 and 14 September in 2026. September 2025 was therefore a clean post-festival month while September 2026 absorbed the full festival — which makes the two months structurally different before a single client is counted. This is why the year-level and trailing-twelve-month comparisons carry more weight in this review than the single-month year-on-year figure.

Known limitations

  • Attribution. 28.7% of the September cohort has no source recorded, and the gap is concentrated in the referral channel, where 48.3% of non-converting clients are unsourced against 24.0% for trials. Channel conclusions are drawn from the attributed majority.
  • The intro pack. A 2-for-1 buyer's second class is pre-paid and is not a repeat-purchase decision. Charts and tables elsewhere in this report use the ledger's own visit counts; where a genuine second-visit rate is quoted it excludes the 11 pre-paid redemptions in the September cohort. The studio should adopt "third visit" as the standard definition of a returning client for anyone who entered on the pack.
  • Trainer attribution. "First-class trainer" is the instructor who took a client's very first visit, and it is the only trainer link the data supports. It does not follow that a trainer caused a client's outcome; it does mean the relationship is measurable and worth monitoring.
  • Lead stage names. Drop-off reasons are drawn from the 2,572 Bandra leads that have closed as lost with a reason recorded. A further 4,244 non-won leads remain in open or in-progress stages and are excluded from that table.
  • Value, not margin. Revenue figures are lifetime value as recorded at the snapshot. No cost, CAC or contribution-margin data is present, so payback periods cannot be calculated.
  • Cohort age. The September cohort is 11 to 41 days old and its lifetime value will keep growing. The ₹315,243 shortfall is a projection against normal yield, not a settled figure; the near-term, first-purchase portion of it is about ₹116,974.
  • Legacy records. 8,711 "Import Visits" placeholder rows (dated 2020-01-01) are excluded throughout. 182 members appear twice where a placeholder duplicates a tracked record.
  • Excluded from scope. This report covers first-visit acquisition and the resulting member value. It does not analyse class-fill rates, capacity utilisation, retail or membership renewals, which need separate data.
