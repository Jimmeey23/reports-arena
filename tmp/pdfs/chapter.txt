What sat behind it did not. September 2026 banked ₹15,29,410 across 196 transactions — a fall of just 2.3% on September 2025 and, read on its own, an unremarkable month. The average sale actually rose, from ₹7,903 to ₹7,803 , and the median sale rose from ₹1,770 to ₹1,942. On the headline numbers this is a steady month. Three things underneath it are not steady, and they are what this report is about. The one-line version, if you read nothing else. The studio served 394 members this September against 305 last September —
its widest September base in three years — and collected ₹3,316 of class revenue from each of them against ₹3,218 . Both of those went up.
The only thing that went down is how often each of them came: 3.56 visits against 4.84 . That single change is worth more than the entire year-on-year
movement in the cash line, and it is the difference between a studio running at capacity and one running at 42.6%. First, the month was funded by people the studio already had. Members making their first-ever Bandra purchase contributed ₹3,00,229, or 19.6% of the month; existing members contributed the other ₹12,29,182. That is normal for a mature studio, and it is genuinely good news about the base. It also means September’s cash figure tells you almost nothing about September’s acquisition performance. The two must be read separately. A note on that number: 19.6% counts members whose first purchase at Bandra was in September. The performance review quotes 12.5% on a different and narrower definition — clients whose first visit was in September, who paid a narrower sum. The two measures diverge because some September visitors had already bought a package, and some September buyers had already visited. Both are correct; the purchase-based figure is the relevant one for a sales report. Second, the commitment length is shortening. Three-month memberships sold 18 units in April 2025 and 2 in September 2026 . One-month memberships fell from 29 units to 15 over the same year. The products that give the studio a forward revenue cushion are being sold less often than they were. Third — and this is the finding with the most consequence — the studio is now selling almost exactly what it delivers. In September 2024, ₹23,96,991 of cash translated into ₹7,73,072 of attended classes: 32% . In September 2025 that rose to 63%. In September 2026 it reached 85% . That is a profound structural change and it deserves to be named plainly, because it cuts both ways. The same flat top line means something completely different in 2026 than it did in 2024 Two years ago a large share of the cash the studio took was never drawn down as classes. Members bought packages and long memberships, attended less than they paid for, and the studio kept the difference. That unused capacity was quiet, real revenue — and it was also a symptom of disengagement. By September 2026 that cushion has largely gone: 85 of every ₹100 banked is now delivered as an attended class , against 32 two years earlier. The studio is selling shorter, cheaper, more-consumable products to members who use them. The revenue is more honest. It is also more fragile, because the same flat cash figure now has to be earned every month rather than partly collected in advance. This is the single most useful reframe in the report. Bandra’s September problem is not that revenue collapsed — it did not. It is that the business model quietly shifted from selling commitment to selling consumption , while the cash total stayed flat and therefore hid the change. Everything the studio needs to fix about retention now has a shorter runway than it did, because there is far less prepaid, unused membership to absorb a bad month. The second job is the one that compounds. Bandra’s membership line is being held up by one-month products while the longer commitments — the ones that roll over less often and generate far more lifetime value per conversation — shrink to almost nothing. Three-month memberships are the clearest case: 18 units in April 2025, 2 units in September 2026 . In the same window one-month units fell from 29 to 15. Only the new six-month and annual tiers grew, and from a base of almost zero. This is a sales-mix problem, not a demand problem — the members are still buying; they are buying shorter. Why this matters more than the flat top line suggests A one-month member makes twelve purchase decisions a year. A three-month member makes four. A twelve-month member makes one. Every step down in commitment length multiplies the number of moments at which the studio can lose the member — and the data shows September losing them in exactly those moments. Combined with a delivery ratio that has risen from 32% to 85%, the studio now has both a shorter commitment base and less prepaid cushion to absorb the consequences. What the first purchase predicts Nothing in the sales ledger separates members as sharply as the product they started on — except how quickly they came back. Take the 4,218 people whose first visit to Bandra is on record and group them by the first thing they bought. The spread in eventual lifetime value is
not marginal, it is structural. A member who started on a 1 Month Unlimited is worth ₹74,283 to the studio . One who started on a Single Class is worth ₹13,689 . Between them sit every other product in the range, in a consistent order: memberships first,
packages second, single classes last. The obvious caution applies, and it is worth stating plainly: members who chose a bigger commitment were already more committed. Four are pricing and structure. The last four attack frequency, channel and cross-selling — the levers that do not require a single new customer. Each recommendation carries the reasoning behind it and the impact the studio should expect, so it can be argued with rather than simply accepted. 01 Re-weight the product ladder toward 12-class packages Sales + Studio Manager Reasoning The 12-class package sells at ₹1,444 a class — the best value the studio offers — and it grew +₹68,740 on four more sales. The 10-class package costs the member ₹1,735 a class and sold 13 units at almost exactly the same average ticket. Both price at roughly ₹17,300. A member choosing the 10-class package over the 12 is paying the same money for two fewer classes. Expected impact Moving 6 of September’s 13 ten-class sales up to the twelve-class tier adds 12 class credits to the studio’s delivered base at no revenue cost, and is the cleanest available improvement to the entry offer. On September volume that is worth about ₹1.1 lakh of delivered capacity a year. 02 Put a named owner on every repeat sale Studio Manager Reasoning 41 of September’s 139 paying members bought more than once — and they produced 34.1% of the month’s ₹15,29,410. Meanwhile ₹3,66,511 of September sales — 24% — have no salesperson recorded, the largest single block in the ledger. Expected impact Named attribution on the unattributed block would make the repeat-purchase cycle visible and manageable for the first time. Selling one additional package to each of the 103 single-purchase members at September’s median ticket of ₹1,942 is worth roughly ₹1.9 lakh a month. 03 Fix the three-month membership before it disappears Sales + Finance Reasoning Three-month memberships ran at 18 units in April 2025 and 2 units in September 2026 . Over the same period one-month units fell from 29 to 15. Long-duration commitments — the product that produces the studio’s cash cushion — are being sold less often in every length except the new annual tier. Expected impact Recovering even 8 three-month units a month at the historic average ticket of about ₹24,900 returns roughly ₹24 lakh a year of higher-commitment revenue, and materially improves the deferred-revenue position. 04 Standardise discount depth at 15% and stop it leaking past it Finance + Front Desk Reasoning 31 September sales carried a discount worth ₹1,45,209 in total — 8.7% of list value. Depth averages 12.8%, but the annual membership was discounted 19.2% and the 20-class pack 19.2%, against 14.4% on the standard packages. Expected impact Holding every discount to the 15% standard while keeping the same volume saves roughly ₹1.1 lakh a year on September’s pattern alone. Depth discipline is also the single easiest lever to audit. 05 Treat the single-class price as a deliberate tier, not a default Sales Reasoning Single classes at ₹1,943 each are now the second-largest unit line at 38 sales, up +₹20,799 year on year. They are profitable per head but they never build a habit, and ₹1,943 a class is the worst value the member can pick. Expected impact Converting a third of September’s 38 single-class buyers into a four-class package at the point of purchase adds about ₹56,000 of committed revenue from the same people, and gives them a reason to return. 06 Protect the new long-duration products Sales + Finance Reasoning September produced the studio’s first ₹1,75,560 annual membership and a ₹1,02,344 six-month sale — together ₹2,77,904, or 18.2% of the month, from two transactions. Both are new propositions and neither existed in September 2025. Expected impact An annual and a six-month sale replace roughly 15 one-month renewals. If these two products can be sold four times a month rather than once, they add about ₹11 lakh a year while lengthening the average commitment. 07 Make the 213 sales count for retention, not just cash CRM Reasoning 139 members paid the studio in September, against 143 new clients acquired. The two numbers are close, which means the month’s acquisition effort is not yet compounding into the paying base — and the existing base contributed 80.4% of the ₹15,29,410. Expected impact Every September buyer who is not booked into a class within 14 days is a renewal risk. A checkout prompt to book the next class is the cheapest retention intervention available and costs nothing to build. 08 Attack frequency, not price — the money is in the fourth visit Studio Manager + CRM Reasoning The check-in ledger shows the base widening to 394 members while visits per member fell to 3.56 from 4.84. In the same ledger, members who trained in both Septembers cut their own frequency 35%, costing 252 visits — 18% of everything filled this month. Cash per member actually rose, from ₹3,218 to ₹3,316. Expected impact This is the only lever in the report that does not require new customers. Lifting the 98 members who came exactly once this month to two visits would add about 98 attendances — roughly ₹91,238 — without a single rupee of acquisition spend. 09 Make the online checkout the default for every membership Sales + Finance Reasoning Stripe sales averaged ₹12,820 a transaction against ₹4,780 for cash, and split-payment sales averaged ₹13,628. Stripe transactions fell from 53 to 28 year on year while counter sales rose. The high-value products are migrating off the channel that handles them best. Expected impact Routing every membership and multi-class pack through a card-on-file or link-based checkout removes the desk as a bottleneck for the products that need a considered decision, and makes the second purchase a one-tap action rather than a visit. 10 Put all discount codes under one owner Finance Reasoning Two codes — NC-2026-SHQ and ZAP10 — account for ₹96,009 of the ₹1,45,209 given away in September, on 34 of the month’s 213 sales. A further single use of 20OFFSHQ cost ₹41,800. Meanwhile the unattributed salesperson block carried the deepest average discount of any seller at 16.3%, against 12.8% for Imran Shaikh. Expected impact Codes are created by whoever needs one, and nobody compares them. One owner, one register, one monthly review of depth by code makes the discount line controllable for the first time and would have caught the 20OFFSHQ depth before it was granted. 11 Sell the second category Studio Manager Reasoning 83% of September buyers bought from a single product category and only 23 of 139 shopped across two. The top ten buyers produced 38% of the month’s cash while the bottom half produced 7.3%. A boutique studio lives on the second and third purchase, not the first. Expected impact A single bundle — one Barre class, one Cycle class, priced to be obviously better than either alone — is the cheapest way to move members out of a single format. Members who train in two formats are the studio’s most durable customers, and on this month’s data almost nobody is being asked to become one. 12 Lead with the membership, not the single class Sales + Front Desk Reasoning A first-time buyer who starts on a 1 Month Unlimited goes on to be worth ₹74,283 ; one who starts on a Single Class is worth ₹13,689 . There are 200 people in that bottom group and 172 in the top one. Expected impact Both are sold at the same desk. Presenting the commitment first — and treating the trial entry as a route into it rather than as a purchase in its own right — changes the mix without touching the price list. 13 Book the second class before the first one ends Front Desk + Sales + CRM Reasoning Members who took their second class inside a week are worth ₹27,745 ; those who waited a month or never returned are worth ₹13,918 . There are 401 people in that last group alone. Expected impact This is a front-desk action, not a campaign: at the end of every first class, before the client leaves the building, the next visit is chosen and booked. It costs nothing, it is measurable within a week, and on this data it is the highest-value habit the sales and service teams could adopt. Method and limitations How to read these numbers, and what they cannot show Sources and method Three exports at a snapshot of 11 October 2026. Sales – Raw (25,027 succeeded, non-voided payments network-wide, 6,540 sale lines from 5,857 payments at Bandra) provides cash received, product, discount, salesperson and payment method. Day End Report – Part 3 – Sessions (25,675 class occurrences) provides revenue attributed to attended classes. Day End Report – Part 1 – New (21,171 member records) identifies which September revenue came from clients acquired that month. What the two revenue measures mean, and why both are shown Cash is money actually received on a payment date; it is what the studio banked. Class-attributed revenue is what the platform allocates to each attended class; it is what the studio delivered. They are different numbers on purpose, and the gap between them is the most informative thing in this report — it measures how much of what members paid for has not yet been consumed. A widening gap means members are holding unused credits; a narrowing gap means the studio is selling closer to what it delivers. Known limitations No cost of sale. No CAC, contribution margin or payroll cost is present in this data, so payback and profitability cannot be calculated. All figures are gross value. Salesperson cover. 24% of September revenue has no salesperson recorded. Attributed performance is therefore a floor on each named seller, not a full picture. Retail is understated in scope. Retail appears here as a revenue line only; no stock, cost or sell-through data is available. Comparability. September 2025 followed Ganesh Chaturthi (27 August); September 2026 absorbed it (14 September). Single-month year-on-year comparisons carry that distortion.
