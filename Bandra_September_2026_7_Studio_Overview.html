Start here

Four numbers describe the studio. Two are improving, one has stalled, and one has never been managed at all.

Margin is improving. Revenue attributed to attended classes rose from ₹9,81,453 to ₹13,06,451 on 14 fewer classes, and revenue per class rose from ₹3,595 to ₹5,044. Revenue per attendance rose from ₹665 to ₹931. The average September buyer spent ₹11,003 against ₹10,432 a year ago.

Discipline is improving. Late cancellations fell from 658 to 199, and the share of the board that was ever booked at all fell from 60.5% to 48.7% — which sounds like bad news and is, but it is honest news: the studio is no longer counting seats nobody intended to use.

Acquisition has stalled. Bandra created 189 leads in September 2026, against a 2026 monthly average of 211 and a 2025 average of 198. Of every lead the studio has ever recorded for this location, 12.9% became a member and 18.4% are retained today.

Frequency has never been managed. 327 memberships are live, worth ₹85,62,873 of recorded cash. 117 of those members, holding ₹23,65,012, have not been in the studio for fifteen days or more. No one is currently responsible for that list, and it is the largest recoverable number in this report.

The single sentence version

The studio has learned to earn more from every seat and to lose fewer of them at the last minute. What it has not yet learned is who its members are, when they are about to stop, and how to reach them before they do — and every one of the three is already visible in the data it holds.

The member base

The studio has more members than a year ago and fewer visits. Both halves of that sentence matter.

This is the most important correction in the report, and it is why a single attendance number is a dangerous thing to manage from. 394 members trained at Bandra in September 2026, against 305 a year earlier and 355 the year before. The base has widened by 29.2% in twelve months and is at its widest in three years.

Attendance fell anyway, from 1,476 to 1,404. The reason is arithmetic: visits per member fell from 4.84 to 3.56. The studio did not lose its audience. It lost its audience’s frequency, and the two are managed in completely different ways.

The distribution behind that average explains the whole of the month. 205 of 394 members came exactly once — 52% of the base — and the members who came ten times or more, 37 people, delivered 572 attendances between them — 41% of the month.

The returning core

78 people were in the room in both Septembers. Between them they account for a fifth of everything the studio filled this year.

The 78 members who trained at Bandra in both September 2025 and September 2026 made 713 visits last year and 461 this year — a fall of 35%, and 252 visits that simply did not happen. That is 18% of every seat the studio filled in September 2026.

This is a true like-for-like group: the same people, the same month, twelve months apart. Had they held their year-ago frequency, September 2026 would have recorded 1,656 attendances — ahead of last September’s 1,476, not behind it. The month’s decline is not an acquisition failure at all. It is a frequency failure among the members who already know the studio.

Who left is a second, quieter finding. Of the 227 members who trained last September and did not appear this September, 126 had made exactly one visit in that month. On lifetime numbers the distinction is stark: the members who stayed had averaged 161 career visits; the ones who left had averaged 34. And the replacements are working harder than the departures did — the 316 members with no September 2025 visit delivered 943 attendances this month, against the 763 left behind by the 227 who departed.

What this reframes

Acquisition is not the problem this report is about. The studio recruited 316 members this September, more than enough to cover its losses of 227. The problem is that both the arrivals and the regulars are coming fewer times each, and no measure the studio currently reports would show that.

What the memberships look like

The studio sells two products in volume, and both of them are designed to end.

Everything the studio has sold as a membership at Bandra is in this ledger: 3,974 paid memberships from 1,699 people, worth ₹4,40,74,830. The four largest products account for 71% of every membership sold, and every one of them is built to end quickly: Single Class 989 units at 5% live, Newcomers 2 For 1 819 units at 5% live, 1 Month Unlimited Membership 575 units at 8% live, 12 Class Package 362 units at 13% live.

The product that keeps its members is not the product that sells in volume. Annual Unlimited Membership renews or stays live 68% of the time, the best rate in the range, and it is bought by 28 people. The four volume lines together have produced 2,745 memberships and between 5% and 13% of them are alive.

Read as a portfolio rather than a menu, the studio is selling short commitments at scale and long ones in ones and twos. Its renewal rate is not a property of its customers. It is a property of its product list, and the list is the easiest thing on this page to change.

Across the whole product book the pattern is consistent: what predicts whether a member stays is how long the product was designed to last, not how much it cost. The products with the longest commitment renew best and the products sold as a quantity of classes renew worst, at every price point in the range.

The acquisition engine

Nearly half of everything the studio attracts arrives through a channel that converts at a third of the studio’s average.

The CRM records a channel on every lead, and the shape of the funnel is unambiguous. 3,714 of 7,822 Bandra leads — 47% — arrive through website and online sources, and they convert at 8.9%. Calls and messages bring 881 leads and convert 25.3%; referrals bring 1,039 and convert 22.7%. By lifetime value the gap is wider still: ₹4,120 against ₹10,185.

The named sources tell the same story with more precision. Of the sources carrying forty or more leads, the largest is Website at 3,090 leads and 8.1% conversion. The best is Incoming sms. Across every one of them the pattern holds: the studio is buying reach and being paid in enquiries it cannot close.

What those leads came for is also recorded, and it is one of the most complete demand signals the studio holds. Barre 57 is named on 5,508 leads and PowerCycle on 2,014. Both convert at almost the same rate (13.2% and 12.7%), and between them they account for 97% of stated demand.

The funnel, end to end

Lead to trial to member to retained. Every stage is measured and every stage leaks.

Across every lead the studio has recorded for Bandra, 44.7% complete a trial and 12.9% become a paying member. On the studio’s own retention field, 18.4% are retained today.

The stage list is a useful place to see where the file actually sits. 2,120 leads are open — more than twice the number that have ever been won — against 1,006 won, 1,083 unresponsive and 718 disqualified. The open file has no owner and no expiry.

Where the money comes from

A year of sales, concentrated enough to be fragile and personal enough to be managed.

In the twelve months to 30 September, 1,015 people bought something at Bandra, for ₹2,05,29,088. The ten largest buyers account for 10.1% of that, the top fifty for 36%, and the half of buyers who spent least for 4.9%. And 548 of those buyers made exactly one purchase in the year — 54% of the file.

September’s own money tells a similar story at smaller scale: 139 buyers, ₹15,29,410, an average of ₹11,003 each. The month’s cash was spread across 7 product categories, led by Memberships at ₹6,66,610, Class Packages at ₹6,10,017, Sessions/Single Classes at ₹1,84,905, Newcomers Special at ₹48,562.

The room

The studio owns the timetable and pays for it whether or not anyone comes. Two days and two hours carry the waste.

September offered 3,293 seats and filled 1,404 of them — 42.6% utilisation, up from 41.8% a year ago. But the average hides a deliberate pattern. By hour, the studio runs 30 sessions at 07:00 and fills 26% of their seats, against 36 sessions at 11:00 filling 56%. By day, Wednesday runs 51 sessions at 31% for ₹1,98,235, while Tuesday runs 52 at 44% for ₹2,90,392.

Put together, the ceiling is not a demand problem. If every slot ran at the studio’s own best-format utilisation of 71%, these same 259 classes would have delivered roughly 2,338 attendances instead of 1,404. The studio does not need more demand to reach that; it needs its existing demand pointed at the right hours.

What the data is telling the studio

Six findings that only appear when the ledgers are joined, and that no single report can show.

First, the leak is at the second visit, not the first. The studio is competent at attracting people and poor at keeping them past the first class. Every lead file the studio holds shows the same cliff.

Second, channel choice is worth more than channel volume. Website leads outnumber call leads by 4.2 to one and are worth 2.47 times a call lead. Reallocating effort towards the smaller, warmer channels is the cheapest growth available.

Third, the live book is a pipeline, not a report. 327 live memberships and 117 quiet members holding ₹23,65,012 is a call list, dated and contactable, that no one currently owns.

Fourth, the studio’s reporting measures results and ignores leading indicators. Attendance, revenue and member counts all describe a month that has finished. Bookings, leads, response time and visits per active member all move first, and all four are already recorded.

Fifth, demand is stated explicitly and is not being used. Almost every lead names the class it wants. Almost every member has a favourite format and a favourite time recorded on their membership. The studio holds a written record of what its audience wants and schedules against format families instead.

Sixth, the studio is far better run than its reporting suggests. Revenue per class up 40%, late cancellations down 70%, cash per buyer up 5%. The problems in this report are problems of placement and follow-through, not of performance.

Recommendations

Twelve actions, ordered by the size of the value each releases
01

Rebalance acquisition away from the website and toward the phone

Marketing + CRM

ReasoningWebsite and online sources produced 3,714 leads — 47% of everything the studio attracts — and converted 8.9% of them at a lifetime value of ₹4,120. Calls and messages produced 881 at 25.3% and ₹10,185; referrals produced 1,039 at 22.7% and ₹8,052.

Expected impactOne referral is worth more than two website leads, and one inbound call worth more than two and a half. The studio is not short of leads; it is short of the leads that convert. A visible referral reward and a published phone number at the point of enquiry are the cheapest mix change available.

02

Target the trial at the class people already ask for

Programming + Marketing

ReasoningBarre 57 is the class 71% of Bandra leads ask about (5,508 leads) and it converts at 13.2%. PowerCycle asks occupy 2,014 leads at 12.7%.

Expected impactDemand is stated explicitly on almost every enquiry and the studio is already recording it. Booking the trial into the format the lead asked for, rather than into whatever slot is free, is a scheduling decision that costs nothing and removes the most common cause of a lost first impression.

03

Put a named owner and a target on the unconverted

CRM + Studio Manager

ReasoningOf 7,822 Bandra leads, 1,006 became members and 2,120 are still open. 1,083 are marked unresponsive and 718 disqualified.

Expected impactThe open file is larger than two years of wins. It has no owner, no target and no date attached to it, and it is the single largest piece of unrealised value in this report. A monthly review of open leads older than thirty days, with one person accountable, would convert some of it and close the rest honestly.

04

Publish conversion by associate and manage the spread

Studio Manager + CRM

ReasoningThe spread is wide and it is not explained by volume: the strongest associate converts 20.4% of 789 leads, the weakest 9.7% of 575. Median first-response time is between 1 and 3 days across the team.

Expected impactThe highest-converting associate is not the one handling the most leads. Publishing the table monthly, without naming anyone, moves the middle of the distribution; pairing the strongest closer with the largest in-box moves the top.

05

Answer faster, and measure it

Front Desk + CRM

ReasoningThe median first response is 2 days after the enquiry and only 404 leads were answered the same day. 0% of records carry a follow-up date that precedes the enquiry itself, which suggests the field is being back-filled rather than logged.

Expected impactSpeed is the one variable in lead handling that the studio controls completely and has never measured properly. A response-time target, logged automatically, would turn an unmanaged process into a number. Fixing the negative dates first is a prerequisite for trusting any of it.

06

Work the renewal grace list as a live pipeline

CRM + Front Desk

Reasoning177 memberships worth ₹29,91,033 are in a renewal grace period right now, and a further 99 are active. Between them they carry ₹85,62,873 of recorded cash.

Expected impactGrace period is a decision window, not a status. These members have not said no — they have stopped being asked. A named call on each one, with the member’s own attendance and favourite format on the screen, converts the list from administration into pipeline.

07

Call the quiet members before they lapse

CRM

Reasoning117 members holding ₹23,65,012 of recorded cash have not trained in fifteen days or more, and 49 of them have not been in for over a month. Lapsed memberships in this book are worth only ₹9,667 on average against ₹26,186 for live ones.

Expected impactThe studio already holds the two fields that predict a lapse: days since last visit and visits in the last 30. A weekly list of anyone whose 30-day visit count has fallen to zero while their membership is still live is the highest-yield call list the studio can build, and it needs no new data.

08

Cut Wednesday and the early morning, and put the seats where the demand is

Programming + Studio Manager

ReasoningWednesday ran 51 sessions at 31% utilisation for ₹1,98,235; Tuesday ran 52 at 44% for ₹2,90,392. At 07:00 the studio offered 383 seats and filled 101; at 11:00 it offered 474 and filled 265.

Expected impactTwo days and two hours account for most of the unused capacity in the month. Moving sessions out of the weakest day and hour and into the strongest is a timetable edit, reversible after four weeks, and it lifts utilisation without a single new member.

09

Give every new member their second and third visit as a plan

Front Desk + CRM

ReasoningAcross 4,097 Bandra first-timers, 35.4% reach a second visit and 23.5% reach a third. Of those who do come back, 17% return within three days and 51% within a week. Among members whose second visit took a month or more, lifetime value is ₹13,941 against ₹43,407 for those who returned immediately.

Expected impactThe second visit is the transition that decides everything else, and the data says it happens inside a week or not at all. Booking visits two and three before the member leaves the building converts an intention into a diary entry, and a diary entry is the only thing that has ever predicted a fourth visit.

10

Make the unpaid seat visible in the fill rate

Finance + Studio Manager

ReasoningSeptember gave away 225 complimentary attendances — 16.0% of everything delivered — worth ₹2,09,367 at the month’s own revenue per head. Comps are excluded from no denominator in the studio’s reporting.

Expected impactReporting utilisation alongside a paid-utilisation figure would show the studio what its promotions actually cost it in seats. No rule change is needed to start with the number — only a second column.

11

Track the four leading indicators monthly, and nothing else

Management

ReasoningThe studio currently reports attendance, revenue and member counts, all of which describe a month that has already finished. The four numbers that move first are leads created (189 in September), leads converted (6.3%), bookings against capacity (48.7%), and visits per active member.

Expected impactEach of those four can be read within a week of the month ending and each one predicts the others. A single page carrying them, published on the first working day, would give the studio a month of warning instead of a month of explanation.

12

Keep the two things that are working

Studio Manager

ReasoningRevenue per attendance rose from ₹665 to ₹931 and revenue per class from ₹3,595 to ₹5,044. Late cancellations fell from 658 to 199. The average September buyer spent ₹11,003 against ₹10,432 a year earlier.

Expected impactThese are genuine gains, achieved in a softer market, and they are the reason the studio is having a conversation about frequency rather than about survival. They should be named, defended and reported as the standard the rest of the business is measured against.

Sources and method

Six independent sources underpin this report, joined on member ID where they overlap. Lead behaviour comes from the CRM lead master (25,315 records across all centres, 7,822 for Bandra). Membership lifecycle comes from the lapse ledger (8,707 Bandra instances, of which 3,974 are paid). Room and revenue economics come from the Sessions export. Check-in behaviour comes from the Day End Part 4 ledger. Acquisition value comes from the New member file. Payments come from the raw sales export.
Channel and source are the studio’s own CRM taxonomy and are used as recorded; a lead is counted once at creation and its outcome is the status it carries today, so recent months are understated for conversion. Response time is the interval between the lead’s creation and the first of four follow-up dates; the field is populated for 100% of records and 0% carry a date that precedes the enquiry, so it is indicative rather than exact.
Revenue is gross value as recorded at snapshot, not contribution margin. Lapsed-membership cash is the amount recorded against the membership, not revenue attributable to the month it lapsed.
