Fourteen people, one timetable, and three different ways to read them

In September 2026, 14 trainers taught 259 classes to 1,404 attendances and generated ₹13,06,451 of class-attributed revenue — 33% more than the same month a year earlier. That headline is genuinely strong, and it is worth stating before anything else: on the studio’s most important operational measure, this group of trainers improved the business.

Underneath the aggregate, the individual spread is wide and, more usefully, it is explainable. Utilisation ranges from 63% to 29%. Revenue per trainer ranges from ₹22,597 to ₹2,04,536. Lifetimes client conversion ranges from 18.6% to 30.0%.

But the most important finding in this report is a negative one, and it changes how the other numbers should be read. Revenue per attendee is nearly flat across the entire group. Every trainer in the studio is teaching an audience worth roughly the same per head — from ₹783 to ₹1,329, a spread of only 70%. That means the large differences in trainer revenue are not about who the trainer is teaching or what the members are worth. They are about how many people are in the room — and how many people are in the room is a function of which slots the studio gave them.

The honest headline is about the timetable, not the people

When utilisation varies 2.2x but revenue per attendee varies only 70%, the variance is mostly in the assignment of slots. Trainers who hold Saturday mornings and 11:00 weekday slots fill their rooms; trainers who hold 07:30 Mondays and 18:00 Thursdays do not. The studio is currently measuring its trainers with a number that is substantially determined by its own scheduling decisions.

That is the single most actionable finding in this report, and the cheapest to act on: assess trainers on how well they fill the slots they hold, and separately assess whether those slots should exist at all.

Two things complicate that clean story, and this edition adds both. First, the trainer is not irrelevant. In the 24 slots taught by more than one trainer this September — the only fair test the data allows — the median gap between the best and worst trainer teaching the same class at the same hour was 22.3 percentage points. Slot assignment explains most of the variance, not all of it. Second, the studio is paying for the emptiest room. In August it paid ₹5,050 per session, but ₹6,128 for a Cycle session against ₹4,594 for a Barre session — so the format that runs 35% of the timetable at a 35% fill rate is also the most expensive one to run.

Vivaran Dhasmana taught the most classes (39) and generated the most revenue (₹2,04,536), but at 39.6% utilisation he is filling fewer than two seats in five. Atulan Purohit taught 27 sessions — barely two thirds as many — drew the same 205 attendees, and generated ₹1,75,972 at 61.2% utilisation, the best of any trainer with more than 20 sessions.

Measured per class, Atulan Purohit earned ₹6,517 and Rohan Dahima ₹6,638, against Vivaran Dhasmana’s ₹5,245 and Karanvir Bhatia’s ₹4,939. That is a 1.29x difference in what an hour of the same studio’s time produces, before any consideration of cost.

The pattern is consistent and it repeats across the table: the trainers with the fewest sessions are often the ones filling them best. That is not coincidence — it usually means a trainer has been given a small number of good slots, while the highest-volume trainers carry the marginal ones. It is worth being explicit that this is a compliment to nobody and a criticism of nobody. It is what a timetable looks like when slots are allocated by seniority or availability rather than by demand.

The controlled test

Twenty-four slots were taught by more than one trainer in September. Inside those slots, the trainer still moved the room by a median of 22 points.

The scorecard in this report compares trainers who hold different slots, so it cannot separate the trainer from the slot. There is one clean way to do that in the data: find the times in the week that two or more trainers taught the same class, and compare them. 24 such slots exist in September.

The result refines the headline. It is true that slot assignment drives most of the variance — but it is not true that the trainer is irrelevant. Within the same day, hour and format, the gap between the best and worst trainer has a median of 22.3 percentage points, and in 6 of the 24 slots the spread exceeds 30 points. That is a real, repeatable trainer effect, and because both trainers are teaching the same class at the same time of day to the same pool, it cannot be blamed on scheduling.

The largest gaps are instructive: Sun 17:00 Mat 57 ran 100.0% full with Reshma Sharma and 35.7% with Richard D'Costa; Sat 09:30 PowerCycle ran 79.5% with Rohan Dahima against 23.1% with Karanvir Bhatia. But 8 of the 24 slots show a spread of ten points or less — in those, who is teaching genuinely does not matter, and the studio should stop treating those slots as a performance question.

A caution on the two largest rows

In 5 of the 24 slots every trainer taught exactly one session, so their spread rests on a single class each and should not be read as a benchmark. Excluding those, the median spread across the remaining 19 slots is actually higher at 23.1 points, and 10 of the 19 show a gap of more than twenty points. The finding does not depend on the outliers; it is the ordinary case. What the outliers do show is the size of the opportunity when a slot is systematically mis-assigned.

What changed in a year

A redistribution, not a decline

The year-on-year table is where the September story becomes concrete. Attendance moved by more than 100 people in both directions between individual trainers, while the studio total fell only 4.9%. Students did not leave the studio. They moved between its trainers.

On the rising side, Atulan Purohit drew +60 attendees on +6 sessions, and Pranjali Jain +89 on +15 sessions. Rohan Dahima added +64 on +3. On the falling side, Karanvir Bhatia lost 104 attendees and Reshma Sharma lost 134 despite teaching +27 more sessions.

Reshma Sharma’s September, read next to her year

She taught 31 sessions against 4 in September 2025 — the largest increase in teaching load of anyone in the studio — and received 33 of the month’s 143 new clients. Her classes filled 42.3% of capacity and her late-cancellation rate was 5.9%, the lowest of any trainer above 20 sessions. Her lifetime clients convert at 20.9%, against a studio average of 21.9%.

None of that contradicts the finding in the main September review that not one of her 33 September clients returned. Both are true: she absorbed the largest load increase in the studio, held one of the biggest intakes of the year, and that intake converted at zero. The concentration — 23% of a month’s acquisition through one pair of hands with no second look — is the studio’s problem to fix, not hers to carry.

11 recurring slots ran at under 20% utilisation in September. The three worst — Anmol Sharma’s Thursday 18:00, Mrigakshi Jaiswal’s Wednesday 19:30 and Vivaran Dhasmana’s Wednesday 07:30 — drew a combined 19 people from 18 scheduled classes.

This matters for how the studio manages people. A trainer holding three dead slots will show a poor utilisation number no matter how well they teach, and a trainer holding three Saturday mornings will show a good one. Until the slots are separated from the trainer in the reporting, the numbers will keep telling the studio about its schedule while appearing to tell it about its staff.

Anmol Sharma is the counter-example that proves the point

He shows 29.1% utilisation — among the lowest in the studio. He also placed 6 of 14 September clients into a second class, the best client follow-through of any trainer in the month, and his clients averaged 2.7 visits against a studio average of 1.69. His four Monday 07:30 slots filled 11.5%. His Thursday 18:00 slot filled 3.8%.

He is, on the evidence, an unusually effective developer of new members who has been given a set of hours nobody wants to attend. Any performance system that ranked trainers by utilisation alone would have identified him as the problem. That is the failure mode this report exists to prevent.

What the studio pays for a session

Teaching cost per session now sits at roughly the value of the class it delivers — and the most expensive format is the emptiest one.

Payroll is only available to August 2026, so this is context rather than September measurement, but the ratio is the point. In August the studio paid ₹14,29,010 to its 283 sessions across 14 teachers — ₹5,050 of teaching cost per session. In September, class-attributed revenue per session was ₹5,044. Those two numbers are now within a few per cent of each other, and while class-attributed revenue is not the studio’s whole revenue, a direct cost that consumes essentially all of the attributed top line is worth stating plainly.

The composition matters more than the total. Split the payroll by discipline and the price of a session differs sharply: Cycle sessions cost ₹6,128 each, Barre sessions ₹4,594. PowerCycle — the format running 35% of the timetable at a 35.0% fill rate — is the single most expensive thing the studio schedules per session. The studio is paying its highest per-session rate to deliver the product with the lowest occupancy, and its lowest rate to Studio FIT, which fills at 71.0%.

Individual per-session rates span a wide band — from ₹1,632 to ₹6,425 across the 14 people on the August payroll. A spread for experience is normal and defensible; what is not defensible is that it bears no relation to fill rate, which is the outcome the business actually needs. The two trainers paid the most in August were not the two who filled the most seats.

Whose first class becomes a member

Across 3,000 first-time visitors, the trainer who took the first class made a measurable difference to whether there was a third.

For every member whose first ever Bandra class fell from January 2025 onward, we know who taught it and how deep the relationship went. Across all of them, the spread on “did this person reach a third visit” runs from 32.0% to 16.1%. The strongest first-class conversion among high-volume trainers is Mrigakshi Jaiswal at 32.0% on 172 first-timers. Taking only trainers who handled forty or more first classes, so that no rate rests on a handful of visitors, the range is 32.0% down to 18.9% — still a difference of 13 percentage points in the single most consequential moment of a member’s life with the studio.

This is not a quality judgement and should not be read as one. First classes are assigned by timetable convenience, and some trainers receive first-timers in the studio’s hardest slots. What it does establish is that the first class is a distinct skill from teaching a full room, and the studio currently has no mechanism to recognise or reward it. One trainer in this list took 172 first-timers; another took 22, and nobody decided that on purpose.

Recommendations

Fourteen actions, ordered by expected value

The first two are scheduling decisions that require no spend and can be taken before next month’s timetable is published. The rest change how trainers are measured, supported and retained.

01

Give Atulan Purohit and Rohan Dahima more of the week

Studio Manager + Head of Training

ReasoningAtulan Purohit drew +60 more attendees than last September from only +6 more sessions, at 61.2% utilisation — the highest of any trainer above 20 sessions. Rohan Dahima filled 62.7% on 17 sessions, second-highest. Both also sit in the top half on lifetime client conversion (27.6% and 22.8%).

Expected impactBoth are currently under-used relative to their fill. Moving 6 of the studio’s weakest slots to these two, and holding them to their current 61% and 63% utilisation, adds roughly 15 attendances a month against the slots they replace.

02

Review the slots no one attends, whoever is teaching them

Studio Manager

Reasoning11 recurring slots ran at under 20% utilisation in September across 7 trainers. The three worst — Anmol Sharma’s Thursday 18:00 (3.8%), Mrigakshi Jaiswal’s Wednesday 19:30 (10.6%) and Vivaran Dhasmana’s Wednesday 07:30 (14.3%) — drew a combined 19 people from 18 scheduled classes.

Expected impactClearing or rescheduling these slots frees about 259 seat-hours a month and removes the single largest source of the studio’s 49 lean sessions. It also protects the trainers holding them, because a dead slot reads as a performance problem when it is a scheduling one.

03

Separate trainer assessment from slot assessment

Head of Training

ReasoningThe spread across trainers looks alarming — 63% utilisation at the top against 29% at the bottom. But revenue per attendee is nearly flat across the whole group, from ₹783 to ₹1,329. Trainers are not teaching different-value audiences; they are being handed differently-sized rooms at differently attractive times. Anmol Sharma is the clearest example: 7.1% conversion on September clients and the best client follow-through in the studio, while holding four 07:30 Monday slots that filled 11.5%.

Expected impactOne rule: judge a trainer on utilisation of the slots they hold, not their raw fill rate. Applied for one quarter, it separates coaching problems from scheduling problems and stops the studio losing good instructors over dead hours.

04

Stop the September concentration from repeating

Studio Manager

ReasoningReshma Sharma received 33 of September’s 143 new clients — 23% of the month’s intake, more than double any other trainer, and none of them returned for a second class. Across the studio, the top three trainers by session volume taught 97.0 of 259 classes (37%).

Expected impactCap any trainer’s share of first-time clients at 15%, and route every new client to a second trainer and a second format. The concentration finding is set out in full in the September performance review; this recommendation is the standing rule that prevents it recurring.

05

Put trainer-level conversion into the monthly review

Head of Training + CRM

ReasoningPayroll records show Bandra’s combined new-client conversion falling from 25.5% in September 2025 to 14.2% in August 2026 — the last month for which payroll data exists. Within the same table individual trainers range from 0% to 100%. Pranjali Jain converted 42.9% of new clients with 57.1% retention in August; three trainers converted none.

Expected impactA single monthly table of trainers by new clients seen, converted and retained would have surfaced both the studio-level decline and the individual spread several months earlier. It costs nothing to produce from data the studio already holds.

06

Give the newer trainers a defined client-handover role

Head of Training

ReasoningFour trainers who barely taught in September 2025 now hold meaningful volume — Reshma Sharma (31 sessions), Pranjali Jain (23), Anmol Sharma (21) and Rohan Dahima (17). Between them they drew 495 attendances. Their lifetime client conversion rates (21.2% for Pranjali, 18.6% for Anmol) sit below the studio’s established trainers.

Expected impactA structured pairing — every new client taught by a developing trainer gets one follow-up class with an experienced one — costs no acquisition spend and directly addresses the second-visit gap that the main review values at roughly ₹2 lakh a month.

07

Rebuild the recurring timetable around 98 slots, not 14 trainers

Programming + Studio Manager

ReasoningThe studio currently runs 98 active recurring weekly slots across 14 trainers. Vivaran Dhasmana holds 14 of them and Anisha Shah holds 4. Lifetime fill on those slots ranges from 24% to 57%. Anisha Shah’s 4 slots fill 57% on aggregate; Anmol Sharma’s 8 fill 24%.

Expected impactThe recurring timetable is the studio’s product, and it should be reviewed slot by slot each quarter with utilisation as the criterion — not reassigned wholesale when a trainer joins or leaves. That is the difference between a schedule and an inheritance.

08

Protect the trainers who fill their rooms

Studio Manager

ReasoningRohan Dahima fills 63% and Anisha Shah 62%. Five trainers who carried a large share of 2025 classes took 49% fewer classes this September, and their lifetime clients were worth ₹8,701 each against ₹6,617 for the group that replaced them.

Expected impactRetention of proven instructors is cheaper than recruitment. A quarterly retention conversation anchored on each trainer’s utilisation and client-conversion numbers gives the studio something concrete to manage, and gives the trainer something concrete to be recognised for.

09

Align per-session pay with the room, not the day

Finance + Head of Training

ReasoningIn August the studio paid ₹5,050 per session across 283 sessions. Split by discipline, Cycle sessions cost ₹6,128 each and Barre sessions ₹4,594. PowerCycle runs 35% of the timetable at a 35% fill rate, while Studio FIT fills 71% — so the highest per-session pay is attached to the emptiest product, and individual rates across the payroll span ₹1,632 to ₹6,425 with no relationship to utilisation.

Expected impactA modest re-weighting — holding the pay envelope constant but tying part of it to each session’s fill against its format benchmark — makes the studio’s biggest controllable cost respond to its biggest controllable problem, without cutting anyone’s rate.

10

Give the first class to the trainers who convert it

Head of Training + Studio Manager

ReasoningAcross 2999 first-time visitors since January 2025, the share who reached a third visit ranges from 16.1% to 32.0% by who taught the first class. The top of that range is approached by trainers who each took 172 and 90 first classes, while others took 31 and 444. The assignment is currently a by-product of the rota.

Expected impactIf the bottom-half trainers moved to the group median, the studio would gain roughly 221 additional members reaching a third visit a year — which, at the studio’s own first-to-third conversion economics, is the single highest-value reallocation available that costs nothing.

11

Publish the shared-slot table every month

Head of Training

ReasoningTwenty-four slots were taught by two or more trainers in September. The median gap between the best and worst trainer within the same slot was 22.3 points, and six slots showed more than 30 points. In the remaining slots the gap was small enough that the trainer is not the variable.

Expected impactOne table a month, naming the trainer on each side of every shared slot, ends the argument about whether utilisation differences are the trainer or the timetable. It also gives the studio a fair way to move slots: on evidence, in the same class, at the same hour.

12

Match the premium formats to the rooms they deserve

Programming + Studio Manager

ReasoningPowerCycle is the studio’s most expensive format to run (₹6,128 per session in August against ₹4,594 for Barre) and its least occupied (35.0%). The same product fills 52.6% in its express form and 52.6% at Cardio Barre Express. This is not a demand problem for the format; it is a demand problem for the slot and the room it sits in.

Expected impactRebuilding PowerCycle at express length and eleven-seat rooms — the configuration where every other Cycling variant fills — addresses the two largest cost lines in the report at once: the most expensive sessions to run and the emptiest rooms in the studio.

13

Judge trainers on the client journey, not only the session

Head of Training + CRM

ReasoningOne in four of September’s classes drew two people or fewer, and the trainers who hold those slots show it in their utilisation whatever they do. The measure that separates them is what happens next: 32% of one trainer’s first-time visitors reach a third visit, against 16% of another’s.

Expected impactUtilisation measures who walked into the room and is largely decided by the timetable. The share of a trainer’s first-timers who come back a second and third time measures what the trainer did once they were there, and it is the only trainer metric in this report that predicts the studio’s economics.

14

Rotate one matched slot a month as a live test

Head of Training + Programming

ReasoningAcross the matched slots the median gap is 22 points and 6 of 24 separate by more than thirty. The rest of the spread is the slot. Nobody currently knows which is which, because the timetable is rebuilt by programme rather than by comparison.

Expected impactTaking the single widest matched slot each month and deliberately swapping the two trainers settles the question in four weeks, at no cost, with the studio’s own data. Repeating it through the year turns a one-off analysis into a staffing principle.

Method and limitations

How to read these numbers, and what they cannot show

Sources and method

Four exports, all at a snapshot of 11 October 2026. Day End Report – Part 3 – Sessions (25,675 class occurrences) is the operational record: who taught, which format, at what hour, to how many people. Day End Report – Part 3 – Teacher Recurring (3,294 rows) aggregates the same data per recurring weekly slot and reconciles to the Sessions file exactly. Day End Report – Part 1 – Payroll (1,120 rows) gives the studio’s own per-trainer monthly conversion and retention record. Day End Report – Part 1 – New (21,171 member records) supplies the client-outcome view: what happened to the members each trainer taught first.

The three lenses, and why all three are needed

An instructor can be read three ways and the three readings disagree often enough to matter. Operationally — how many classes, how full, how much revenue. Commercially — the studio’s own payroll conversion and retention record per trainer. By client outcome — what the members a trainer taught first went on to be worth. This report shows all three side by side, because a trainer can fill a room and still produce clients who never return, and a trainer can hold dead slots and still be the best developer of new members in the studio.

Known limitations

  • Payroll stops in August 2026. No September payroll record exists, so the studio’s own conversion and retention numbers cannot be shown for the month under review. Where payroll figures appear they are labelled with their month.
  • Small samples. Several trainers taught under 10 sessions in September and three taught fewer than five. Their percentages are shown for completeness but should not be read as performance signals; the workbook flags sample sizes.
  • Attribution is not causation. "First-class trainer" is the instructor who took a client’s very first visit, and it is the only trainer link the data supports. Clients choose classes as well as trainers, so the relationship is measurable but not causal.
  • No trainer cost. This data contains no pay rates or cost per class, so revenue is gross and no margin per trainer can be calculated.
  • The recurring-slot fill rates in the final table are lifetime figures across each slot’s full history, not September-only, and are labelled as such.
